<Step title="Same line, same MSE: how do we choose the next parameters?">

In [linear regression](/learn/foundations/linear-regression/), our model predicted a number with $\hat y = wx+b$. We measured its mistakes with **mean squared error (MSE)**: subtract the observed answer from each prediction, square those errors, then average them. We will keep that exact model, those twelve examples, and that loss throughout this lesson.

$$L(w,b)=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{12}(wx_i+b-y_i)^2.$$

Here $L$ names the loss, $i$ identifies an example, and $\sum$ means "add the twelve terms." **Linear regression is the model. MSE is the score we want to lower. Gradient descent is the procedure that changes the model's parameters to lower that score.**

The map below is the regression lesson's loss map. Its horizontal coordinate is $w$, its vertical coordinate is $b$, and each contour joins parameter pairs with the same MSE. The dot is one whole line's parameter pair, not one training example. The diamond marks the best line.

At the start, $w=-1.4$ and $b=-2.2$, so MSE is **28.664**. Play the animation and watch the dot approach the diamond. The following steps explain how each move is calculated.

</Step>

<Step title="A gradient tells us which changes lower MSE">

Before choosing a move, ask two questions: what happens to MSE if we increase $w$ a little while holding $b$ fixed, and what happens if we increase $b$ while holding $w$ fixed? These two rates of change are **partial derivatives**. Together they form the **gradient**, written $\nabla L=(g_w,g_b)$.

To calculate them, start with the error of example $i$: $e_i=wx_i+b-y_i$. The derivative of its square is $2e_i$. Changing $b$ changes the error at rate 1; changing $w$ changes it at rate $x_i$. Multiply those rates, then average over the examples:

$$g_w=\frac{2}{12}\sum_i e_i x_i,\qquad g_b=\frac{2}{12}\sum_i e_i.$$

At the starting line, adding the twelve error-times-input terms gives $\sum_i e_i x_i\approx-105.016349$; adding the errors gives $\sum_i e_i\approx-33.549238$. Therefore:

$$g_w\approx\frac{2}{12}(-105.016349)\approx-17.502725,$$
$$g_b\approx\frac{2}{12}(-33.549238)\approx-5.591540.$$

A **negative** derivative means a small increase in that parameter lowers MSE. Both derivatives are negative here, so our next move should increase both parameters. For example, increasing only $w$ by $0.01$ lowers MSE from $28.664$ to $28.490$.

Reset the figure and read the two slopes beneath it. The magenta arrow points opposite the gradient. The side chart takes a slice through the map along that direction: at distance zero we are at the update's starting parameters; moving a little to the right lowers MSE.

<GradientDescent2D client:visible surface="regression" lockSurface view="slice" title="Use the two MSE slopes to choose a direction" />

<details>
<summary>Why subtract the gradient?</summary>

For small changes, $\Delta L\approx g_w\Delta w+g_b\Delta b$. If we choose $\Delta w=-\eta g_w$ and $\Delta b=-\eta g_b$, then $\Delta L\approx-\eta(g_w^2+g_b^2)$, which is negative when $\eta>0$ and the gradient is nonzero. This is a local approximation: a large step can overshoot, as we will see below.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Turn the direction into one complete update">

The gradient supplies the direction and scale of change. The **learning rate**, $\eta$ ("eta"), controls how much of that change we take. With $\eta=0.08$, the update rule is:

$$w_{\mathrm{new}}=w-\eta g_w,\qquad b_{\mathrm{new}}=b-\eta g_b.$$

Substitute the starting parameters and the slopes we just calculated:

$$w_{\mathrm{new}}\approx-1.4-0.08(-17.502725)\approx0.000218,$$
$$b_{\mathrm{new}}\approx-2.2-0.08(-5.591540)\approx-1.752677.$$

Subtracting a negative number increases the parameter. Calculate **both gradients at the old parameters**, then update $w$ and $b$ together. Now make all twelve predictions again using the new parameters, square the new errors, and average:

$$L_{\mathrm{new}}\approx\frac{1}{12}\sum_i(0.000218x_i-1.752677-y_i)^2\approx9.073.$$

The MSE fell from **28.664 to 9.073**. That comparison is how we check whether the move helped. Watch the slow update below: the calculation states its destination while the dot moves there. The current MSE readout follows the moving dot. Displayed arithmetic is rounded; the symbol $\approx$ means "approximately equal."

</Step>

<Step title="Recalculate the gradient after every update">

The slopes change when the parameters change. We must therefore repeat the whole cycle: **predict, calculate errors, calculate gradients, update parameters**. Reusing the first gradient would keep pushing in the original direction even after we passed the best line.

The figure now repeats this cycle. At learning rate $0.08$, MSE is **9.073 after one update**, **0.430 after ten**, and about **0.200 after forty**. Scrub to $t=2$ seconds (ten completed updates) and inspect the new slopes. They are calculated from the new line's errors, not the starting line's errors.

The graph shows MSE against update count. Its vertical scale is logarithmic: moving from 10 to 1 is the same vertical distance as moving from 1 to 0.1. Near the best line, the positive and negative contributions to the gradient nearly cancel, so updates get smaller. MSE does not reach zero because a single straight line cannot pass through all twelve points.

<GradientDescent2D client:visible surface="regression" lockSurface view="update" title="Recompute the slopes: the updates shrink near the best line" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code for these MSE updates</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

# x and y contain the same twelve training examples.
w, b = -1.4, -2.2
learning_rate = 0.08
for update in range(48):
    error = w * x + b - y
    gradient_w = 2 * np.mean(error * x)
    gradient_b = 2 * np.mean(error)
    w = w - learning_rate * gradient_w
    b = b - learning_rate * gradient_b
    mse = np.mean((w * x + b - y) ** 2)
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The learning rate can help or undo the progress">

The direction can be sensible while the step is too long. The learning rate multiplies both gradient components: a small rate moves cautiously; a larger one can cross the valley and land on its opposite side.

The figure starts at **0.26**, which zigzags but still approaches the best line. Change the learning rate and compare MSE after 48 updates:

- **0.01:** MSE is **1.296**. The steps are safe but progress is slow.
- **0.08:** MSE is about **0.200**, near the minimum.
- **0.26:** MSE is **0.202**. Overshoots shrink, so the fit still improves overall.
- **0.28:** MSE grows to about **3855**. Overshoots grow instead of shrinking: this is **divergence**.

Try $0.28$ and watch the loss graph turn upward. The dot eventually leaves the map; the graph and readout continue to show its growing error. A downhill direction is only a local guide, not a promise that every step length will reduce loss.

<GradientDescent2D client:visible surface="regression" lockSurface view="rates" lr={0.26} title="Same MSE, different step sizes" />

<details>
<summary>Show the stability boundary for this MSE</summary>

This MSE is a quadratic bowl. Its matrix of second derivatives is $H=2\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{mean}(x^2)&\operatorname{mean}(x)\\\operatorname{mean}(x)&1\end{pmatrix}$. Let $\lambda_{\max}$ be its largest eigenvalue, the greatest curvature in any parameter direction. Full-batch gradient descent converges from every start when $0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$. For these twelve inputs the boundary is approximately **0.2724**, explaining why 0.26 converges and 0.28 diverges. This number belongs to this dataset and loss scale; it is not a universal learning rate.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="SGD estimates the gradient from fewer examples">

So far, each update uses all twelve examples: **full-batch gradient descent**. With a very large dataset, computing every example's contribution before each update can be expensive. **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)** uses a randomly selected example, or a small group called a **mini-batch**, to estimate the gradient.

The model and MSE objective stay the same. Only the examples used to choose an update change. For one sampled example, there is no twelve-term average: its gradient is simply $g_w=2e_i x_i$, $g_b=2e_i$.

Reset this figure. Its first update uses **example 9**, giving slopes approximately **$(-12.9624,-10.2874)$**, compared with the full-data slopes **$(-17.5027,-5.5915)$**. At the same learning rate, its first update gives MSE **10.565**, rather than the full-batch update's **9.073**. The MSE readout still evaluates all twelve examples, so we can compare fairly.

Watch the sampled-example path wobble. One example can prefer a move that makes the average error worse, especially near the best fit. With uniform sampling, the average of the possible sample gradients equals the full gradient; individual updates need not lower the full loss. Larger mini-batches reduce the noise, and decreasing the learning rate can help the parameters settle.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **"Gradient descent is a different model from linear regression."** It is a way to fit parameters. Here it fits the same line using the same MSE.
- **"A negative gradient means decrease the parameter."** The rule subtracts the gradient. Subtracting a negative number increases the parameter.
- **"The gradient points directly at the best parameters."** It describes the local direction of steepest increase. Its opposite can cross a valley rather than point straight at its bottom.
- **"Every gradient-descent update lowers loss."** A large learning rate can overshoot; a sampled gradient can also increase full-data loss.
- **"A zero gradient means zero error."** At the best line, error contributions cancel even though individual predictions are still imperfect.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>Which part is the model, which is the loss, and which is the training procedure?</summary>

The model is $\hat y=wx+b$. The loss is MSE. Gradient descent is the procedure that updates $w$ and $b$ to reduce MSE.

</details>

<details>
<summary>If b = -2.2, its gradient is -5.59154, and the learning rate is 0.08, what is the new b?</summary>

$b_{\mathrm{new}}=-2.2-0.08(-5.59154)\approx-1.752677$. It increases because we subtract a negative value.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why must we recalculate the errors before the second update?</summary>

The new parameters make different predictions, so they have different errors and different MSE slopes. The next gradient must describe the new line.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why can one-example SGD raise the full-data MSE?</summary>

It chooses a move using one example's squared error. Improving that example can worsen enough of the others to raise their average squared error.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Going deeper">

The same update rule applies to neural networks: compute the chosen loss, differentiate it with respect to the parameters, and subtract a scaled gradient. Neural-network losses need not have the single bowl-shaped minimum we have here. They can contain local minima, flat regions, and saddles, where the gradient is zero without a minimum. We will return to those difficulties in [optimizers](/learn/neural-networks/optimizers/).
