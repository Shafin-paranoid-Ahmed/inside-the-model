<Step title="A perfect score that means nothing">

A model can score perfectly on its training data and still be useless. The figure shows why. Fourteen points were sampled from a smooth curve (dashed) with a little random noise added, so none of them sits exactly on the curve. We fit a **polynomial** to them: a curve $\hat y = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + \dots + c_d x^d$. Here $\hat y$ (read "y-hat") is the prediction, $y$ is the observed answer as in [linear regression](/learn/foundations/linear-regression/), and $c_0, c_1, \dots$ are the parameters. The **degree** $d$ is the highest power, and it sets the model's flexibility. A degree-1 polynomial is $c_0 + c_1 x$, the straight line from linear regression with $b = c_0$ and $w = c_1$. A degree-12 polynomial has thirteen parameters and can bend many times.

Keep the three pieces apart. The **model** is the polynomial. The **loss** is the same mean squared error as in linear regression. The **procedure** here is the least-squares shortcut from that lesson, which jumps straight to the parameters with the lowest MSE instead of taking gradient steps. It works for any degree because $x^2, x^3, \dots$ are just extra inputs, each with its own parameter.

Watch the timeline raise the degree from 1 to 12. The fitted curve (solid) bends more and more to reach the points. Each short vertical segment is a training residual, blue above the curve and orange below. Near the end the curve passes almost through every point, yet it swings far from the dashed truth between them.

</Step>

<Step title="Underfitting: too rigid to see the pattern">

At degree 1 the model is a straight line. No choice of slope and intercept can follow an S-shaped curve, so it misses in a systematic way: it is wrong in the same direction wherever it is wrong. This is **underfitting**, and the error it causes is called **bias**.

The tell-tale sign is that the error is high on *both* the training data and fresh data.

**Where the 0.18 comes from.** The best line for these 14 points is $\hat y = -0.051 + 0.764\,x$. The leftmost point has $x = -0.932$ and observed $y = -0.338$:

- prediction: $-0.051 + 0.764 \times (-0.932) = -0.051 - 0.712 = -0.763$
- error: $-0.763 - (-0.338) = -0.426$
- squared error: $(-0.426)^2 = 0.182$

Do the same for all 14 points and the squared errors add up to $2.525$. Divide by 14: the **training error** is $2.525 / 14 = 0.180$. The **test error** is the same calculation on a separate set of 200 points from the same source that the fit never saw: $0.193$. Getting more data would not help: the line is simply not expressive enough.

</Step>

<Step title="The sweet spot">

Raise the degree and the training error drops quickly, then slowly. Degree 3 is enough to follow the S-shape: training error 0.032, test error 0.075. The **test error** is the one that matters, because it measures how well the model handles data it has never seen. Its lowest value in this figure, 0.072, comes at degree 5.

There is a floor below which no model can go. The noise added to each label has variance $0.25^2 = 0.0625$ (the dashed accent line on the chart), and that part of the error is unpredictable by construction. A test error near 0.07 is almost all noise floor.

Drag along the error chart yourself to compare degrees.

<PolyOverfit client:visible degree={3} title="Degree 3: about right" />

</Step>

<Step title="Overfitting: memorizing the noise">

Keep raising the degree. Training error keeps falling, all the way to 0.002 at degree 12, because a more flexible curve can always reach a few more points. But the test error turns around: 0.131 at degree 10, 0.81 at degree 11, and 2.7 at degree 12. The model has started to fit the *noise* in these particular fourteen points, which is useless for any other sample.

This is **overfitting**. The gap between the falling training curve and the rising test curve is the whole story. **A low training error is not evidence of a good model.** Only error on held-out data is. How to hold data out properly is the topic of [train, validation and test sets](/learn/foundations/train-val-test/).

</Step>

<Step title="Variance: the fit depends on which points you got">

Why does the high-degree curve swing so wildly? Because a very flexible model listens to every accident in the data. Draw a different sample of 14 points from the same source, and the curve comes out completely different. That sensitivity is called **variance**.

Press **New data** to resample, or use the tick box to draw the fits from 20 other datasets at once. At low degree the faint curves nearly coincide: the line is stable, but consistently off. At high degree they scatter widely, especially near the edges, even though every one of them fits its own dataset almost perfectly.

</Step>

<Step title="The trade-off, measured">

We can split the expected error of a model into three parts. Fit the same degree to many fresh datasets and average. The **bias** squared is how far the *average* fit is from the truth; the **variance** is how much individual fits scatter around that average; the **noise** is the part no model can remove.

The chart now adds two more lines, computed from 40 independent datasets. Bias² falls from 0.109 at degree 1 to 0.003 at degree 3. Variance climbs the other way, from 0.008 at degree 1 to 0.017 at degree 3, 0.155 at degree 9 and 24 at degree 12.

The expected test error is their sum plus the noise floor. At degree 1: $0.109 + 0.008 + 0.0625 = 0.180$. At degree 3: $0.003 + 0.017 + 0.0625 = 0.083$. Our one dataset measured $0.193$ and $0.075$: close, but not equal, because these three parts are averages over many possible datasets and ours is just one of them. Either way the total is high at both ends and lowest in between. Making a model more flexible trades bias for variance.

<PolyOverfit client:visible decompose degree={3} title="Bias², variance and the noise floor" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Let the data be $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat f_D$ be the model fitted on a random training set $D$. At a fixed point $x$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{D,\varepsilon}\big[(y - \hat f_D(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D(x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_D\big[(\hat f_D(x) - \mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.$$

The figure averages bias² and variance over $x \in [-1, 1]$ and estimates the expectations with 40 datasets.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import chebyshev as C

x = np.linspace(-0.95, 0.95, 14)
y = 0.8 * np.sin(3 * x) + np.random.default_rng(3).normal(0, 0.25, 14)
for d in (1, 3, 12):
    p = C.Chebyshev.fit(x, y, d)         # least squares; Chebyshev form = same curves, steadier arithmetic
    print(d, np.mean((p(x) - y) ** 2))   # train error only ever goes down
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** a model with zero training error is the best possible model. **Actually** zero training error usually means it has memorized noise; judge models on data they were not fitted to.
- **You might think** overfitting means a model is "too big". **Actually** it is about flexibility relative to the amount of data and noise. Degree 12 is fine with 10,000 points and terrible with 14. More data reduces variance.
- **You might think** the most complex model is always the most accurate. **Actually** the simplest model that captures the pattern generalizes best, because each extra knob adds variance.
- **You might think** bias and variance can both be driven to zero. **Actually** for a fixed dataset size they trade off, and the noise floor stays no matter what. In real models, very large networks trained on lots of data can behave differently from this polynomial picture, but the same concepts apply.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>Training error keeps falling as the degree rises. Why is that not a good sign by itself?</summary>

A higher-degree polynomial contains every lower-degree one, so it can always fit the training points at least as well. Training error measures fit to the data you already have, noise included, not how the model will do on new data.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A model has high error on both training and test data. Overfitting or underfitting?</summary>

Underfitting (high bias). An overfit model has low training error and high test error.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does the test error curve have a U shape?</summary>

Test error is bias² plus variance plus noise. Bias² falls as flexibility increases while variance rises, so their sum first falls and then rises.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What can you do about high variance without changing the model?</summary>

Collect more training data, or average several models. Variance comes from the sample being small and noisy. Simplifying or constraining the model also reduces it.

</details>

</Step>
