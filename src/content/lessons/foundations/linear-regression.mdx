<Step title="Start with one prediction">

**Linear regression** fits a straight line to examples so that we can predict a number. Each example gives us an input $x$ and an observed answer $y$. The line produces a prediction, written $\hat y$ ("y hat"):

$$\hat y = wx + b.$$

The **slope** $w$ tells us how much the prediction changes when $x$ increases by 1. The **intercept** $b$ is the prediction at $x = 0$. These are the two **parameters** we can adjust. The observed data stays fixed.

Start with the guess $w = -1.4$, $b = -2.2$. Example 8 in the figure has $x \approx 0.931516$ and observed $y \approx 1.720559$. Multiply its input by the slope, then add the intercept:

$$\hat y \approx (-1.4)(0.931516) + (-2.2) \approx -3.504.$$

The model predicts a negative answer even though the observed answer is positive: this line needs improving. The symbol $\approx$ means "approximately equal"; we round the displayed numbers to keep them readable.

The outlined point is the observed answer; the point on the line directly below it is the prediction. Watch $b$ rise from $-2.2$ to $-1.2$ while $w$ stays fixed. Every prediction rises by 1, including this one, from $-3.504$ to $-2.504$.

<LineFit client:visible mode="prediction" squares={false} title="Multiply x by w, then add b" />

</Step>

<Step title="Turn the predictions into one MSE">

How can we tell whether a new line is better? Keep the **mean squared error (MSE)** from the [loss-functions lesson](/learn/foundations/loss-functions/). Calculate every prediction, square each miss, then average. We will use this same loss throughout the lesson.

For the starting line, example 8 gives:

1. **Prediction:** $\hat y \approx -3.504122$.
2. **Signed error:** prediction minus observed answer, $e = \hat y - y \approx -3.504122 - 1.720559 = -5.224681$. Negative means the prediction is too low.
3. **Squared error:** $e^2 \approx (-5.224681)^2 \approx 27.297$. Squaring makes misses in either direction positive.

The previous lesson called $y - \hat y$ the **residual**. Here we use the opposite sign, $e = \hat y - y$, to make the update formulas easier to read. Their squares are identical: $(-e)^2 = e^2$. The loss has not changed.

Do that for all twelve examples. Their squared errors add to about $343.973$. Divide by twelve:

$$L(w,b) = \text{MSE} = \frac{e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \cdots + e_{12}^2}{12} \approx \frac{343.973}{12} \approx 28.664.$$

Here $L$ is just a short name for the loss. In compact notation, $L(w,b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(wx_i+b-y_i)^2$: $n$ is the number of examples, $i$ picks one example, and $\sum$ means "add these values".

Each bar below is one squared error, and the dashed line is their average. At the start, compare example 8's bar, $27.297$, with the MSE, $28.664$. One point's error is only one part of the score; fitting the line means improving the average across all points.

<LineFit client:visible mode="errors" title="Twelve squared errors become one mean squared error" />

</Step>

<Step title="Changing the line changes the loss">

We now have a chain we can calculate: **choose $w$ and $b$ → predict every $y$ → calculate MSE**. Training means finding values of $w$ and $b$ that make that final number small.

The two plots below show the same choice in two ways. On the left, the axes are input $x$ and answer $y$: one choice of parameters draws a whole line. On the right, the axes are slope $w$ and intercept $b$: that same choice is just one dot. The curves on the right join parameter choices with equal MSE. This is a **contour map of the loss**, like a hiking map whose height is prediction error.

Watch the dot move around the map. The starting line has MSE $28.664$. At the diamond, $w \approx 0.972$ and $b \approx 0.557$, the MSE is about $0.200$. Moving the dot changes the line and its errors together. The map is a picture of the MSE we just calculated, not a new loss function.

<LineFit client:visible mode="tour" title="A line in the data plot is a dot on the MSE map" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use the MSE slopes to improve the line">

Trying every line would be slow. Instead, ask two smaller questions: **if we increase $w$ a little, does MSE rise or fall? What if we increase $b$?** A derivative answers each question by measuring how quickly the loss changes when that parameter changes and the other stays fixed.

Write these two slopes as $g_w = \partial L/\partial w$ and $g_b = \partial L/\partial b$. Together they are called the **gradient**. Notice the distinction: $w$ is the slope of the prediction line; $g_w$ measures how changing that slope affects the MSE.

We can calculate them from the errors we already have. A squared error $e^2$ changes at a rate of $2e$ with respect to $e$. Increasing $b$ changes every prediction, and therefore every error, at a rate of 1. Increasing $w$ changes example $i$'s prediction at a rate of $x_i$. Average those contributions:

$$g_b = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i, \qquad g_w = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i x_i.$$

For our starting line, $w = -1.4$ and $b = -2.2$, the twelve errors sum to about $-33.549$. Multiplying each error by its input and adding gives about $-105.016$. So:

$$g_b \approx \frac{2}{12}(-33.549) \approx -5.592,$$
$$g_w \approx \frac{2}{12}(-105.016) \approx -17.503.$$

Both are negative: a small increase in either parameter lowers the MSE at this starting point. To move downhill, **subtract** a small multiple of each loss slope. That multiplier is the **learning rate**, written $\eta$ ("eta"). Here we choose $\eta = 0.08$:

$$w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta g_w \approx -1.4 - 0.08(-17.503) \approx 0.000,$$
$$b_{\text{new}} = b - \eta g_b \approx -2.2 - 0.08(-5.592) \approx -1.753.$$

Subtracting a negative value increases the parameter. Both gradients are calculated at the **old** line, then both parameters are updated together. Finally, make all twelve predictions again using the new line and recompute the same MSE. It falls from **28.664 to 9.073**.

Watch this single update slowly. The calculation below the figure gives its destination; the dot and line move there together. We have not changed the data or the loss. We changed the parameters because the MSE slopes told us which direction would help.

<LineFit client:visible mode="update" title="One gradient-descent update: MSE 28.664 to 9.073" />

<details>
<summary>Show the derivative one multiplication at a time</summary>

For one example, $e_i = wx_i+b-y_i$. Its squared error is $e_i^2$. The chain rule multiplies the rate at which the square changes by the rate at which the error changes:

$$\frac{\partial(e_i^2)}{\partial b} = \frac{\partial(e_i^2)}{\partial e_i}\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial b} = 2e_i\cdot 1,$$

$$\frac{\partial(e_i^2)}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial(e_i^2)}{\partial e_i}\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial w} = 2e_i\cdot x_i.$$

Add those per-example derivatives and divide by $n$, exactly as we did for the loss. That gives the two MSE-gradient formulas above. If derivatives are unfamiliar, the [derivatives lesson](/learn/math/derivatives/) explains them through small changes on a curve.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Repeat the same calculation: gradient descent">

One update improved the line, but it has not finished fitting it. Recalculate the errors at the new line, recalculate the two gradients, and update again. **Gradient descent** is the name for this repeated procedure. Linear regression tells us what model we are fitting; MSE tells us how wrong it is; gradient descent tells us how to adjust it.

The figure below repeats the update forty times. The MSE falls from $28.664$ to $9.073$ after one update, to $0.430$ after ten, and to $0.200$ after forty. As the fit improves, the gradient approaches zero, so the updates get smaller. Scrub near the end and compare the line with the dashed best-fit line.

<LineFit client:visible title="Repeat: predictions, MSE gradients, new w and b" />

For these data, MSE over $(w,b)$ forms a smooth bowl with one minimum. With a suitable learning rate, descent approaches that minimum. The lowest MSE is above zero because these twelve points do not all lie on a single straight line. A rate that is too large can increase the error instead; choosing the step size is one of the questions for the [gradient-descent lesson](/learn/foundations/gradient-descent/).

<details>
<summary>Show the code for the same MSE updates</summary>

~~~python
import numpy as np

w, b = -1.4, -2.2
learning_rate = 0.08

for update in range(40):
    prediction = w * x + b
    error = prediction - y
    mse = np.mean(error ** 2)

    # Compute both slopes BEFORE changing either parameter.
    gradient_w = 2 * np.mean(error * x)
    gradient_b = 2 * np.mean(error)

    w = w - learning_rate * gradient_w
    b = b - learning_rate * gradient_b

final_mse = np.mean((w * x + b - y) ** 2)
~~~

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="A shortcut for this model: least squares">

There is also a direct way to find the best line. For this model and squared-error loss, we can solve for the parameters where both loss slopes are zero. This is the **least-squares solution**: "least" because we minimize the sum of squared errors. Dividing that sum by twelve does not change which line is best.

The **closed-form** method means evaluating a formula instead of repeating updates. It gives $w \approx 0.972$, $b \approx 0.557$ and MSE $\approx 0.200$, the same destination that gradient descent approaches. The figure shows the line move directly to that answer.

<LineFit client:visible mode="closed" title="A direct formula finds the same minimum MSE" />

The shortcut is useful for linear regression. Gradient descent matters because we can also apply it to models for which no such direct formula is available, including neural networks. Both are ways to fit parameters; neither changes what the model predicts or how MSE scores it.

<details>
<summary>Show the least-squares formula and where it comes from</summary>

Let $\bar x$ be the average input and $\bar y$ the average observed answer. At the minimum, the intercept gradient is zero, so:

$$0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i(wx_i+b-y_i) = w\bar x+b-\bar y.$$

Rearrange to get $b = \bar y-w\bar x$: the fitted line passes through the point of averages. Put this expression for $b$ into the slope-gradient equation:

$$0 = \sum_i [w(x_i-\bar x)-(y_i-\bar y)]x_i.$$

The bracketed terms sum to zero, so replacing the last $x_i$ by $x_i-\bar x$ does not change the sum. Expand and move one term to the other side:

$$w\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2 = \sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y).$$

Therefore, when the inputs are not all identical:

$$w = \frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad b = \bar y-w\bar x.$$

The numerator measures how inputs and answers vary together. The denominator measures how much the inputs vary. Dividing both sums by $n$ gives the shorter name "covariance divided by variance"; it is the same calculation.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** linear regression and gradient descent are two different prediction models. **Actually**, the line is the model; gradient descent is one method for fitting its parameters.
- **You might think** the loss map introduces a new loss. **Actually**, each height is the same MSE, evaluated for another choice of $w$ and $b$.
- **You might think** the slope $w$ and the gradient $g_w$ are the same thing. **Actually**, $w$ relates input to prediction; $g_w$ relates a change in $w$ to a change in MSE.
- **You might think** the best line must pass through every point. **Actually**, it minimizes the average squared miss. Here its MSE is about $0.200$, not zero.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>For x = 2, y = 5, w = 1 and b = 1, what are the prediction, signed error and squared error?</summary>

Prediction: $1\cdot 2+1=3$. Signed error: $3-5=-2$. Squared error: $(-2)^2=4$. This is one example's contribution; MSE averages the squared errors over all examples.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Three squared errors are 1, 4 and 9. What is the MSE?</summary>

$(1+4+9)/3=14/3\approx 4.667$. Add the squared errors, then divide by the number of examples.

</details>

<details>
<summary>If w = 1, the MSE gradient with respect to w is −3, and the learning rate is 0.1, what is the new w?</summary>

$w_{\text{new}}=1-0.1(-3)=1.3$. The negative gradient says a small increase in $w$ lowers the loss here.

</details>

<details>
<summary>After updating w and b, do we change the labels or choose another loss?</summary>

Neither. Keep the observed labels and MSE. Use the new parameters to make fresh predictions, calculate fresh errors and gradients, then take the next update.

</details>
