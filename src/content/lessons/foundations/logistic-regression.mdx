<Step title="A line can't answer yes or no">

Sixteen points sit on a number line $x$. Each belongs to one of two classes: class 1 (blue) or class 0 (orange). Class 1 tends to sit on the right, class 0 on the left, but the two overlap in the middle. Given a new $x$, we want an answer to "which class?" together with a confidence, such as "70% likely class 1".

[Linear regression](/learn/foundations/linear-regression/) gives us a score $z = wx + b$, but a score can be $-3$ or $12$, and neither is a probability. **Logistic regression** fixes this by passing the score through a squashing function, the **sigmoid**, which turns any number into something between 0 and 1.

The left panel shows the sigmoid curve in teal. The right panel is a loss map like the ones in the previous lessons: every choice of $(w, b)$ is one curve. Watch the timeline. The model starts as a poor guess and trains until the curve sits where the classes change.

</Step>

<Step title="The sigmoid squashes any score into (0, 1)">

The sigmoid is

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.$$

For a very negative $z$, $e^{-z}$ is huge and $\sigma(z)$ is close to 0. For $z = 0$, $\sigma(0) = 1/2$. For a very positive $z$, $e^{-z}$ is tiny and $\sigma(z)$ is close to 1. The curve is smooth and always increasing, so a larger score always means a higher probability.

Our model is $P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(wx + b)$. In the figure a probe slides along $x$ at the fitted values $w = 1.26$, $b = 0.44$. At $x = -4$ the score is $-4.61$ and the probability is 0.010. At $x = 0$ the score is $0.44$ and the probability is 0.61. At $x = 4$ it reaches 0.996. The dotted lines read the probability off the curve.

</Step>

<Step title="w tilts the curve, b slides it">

The two knobs have simple jobs. The **offset** $b$ slides the curve left or right: the curve crosses 0.5 exactly where $wx + b = 0$, at $x = -b/w$. This is the **decision boundary**, the dashed vertical line. With the fitted values it is at $x = -0.35$. Points to its right are predicted class 1.

The **steepness** $w$ sets how quickly confidence grows as you move away from the boundary. Small $w$ gives a lazy ramp; large $w$ gives a sharp step.

The timeline turns one knob at a time. First only $b$ moves: the curve slides left and right without changing shape, and the dashed boundary slides with it. Then only $w$ moves: the curve flattens and sharpens. On the map, the dot travels straight up and down, then straight left and right. Afterwards, drag the sliders or click anywhere on the contour map to try your own settings.

The ring on the curve above each point is that point's predicted $P(y = 1)$. The pink stem joins it to the point's true label (1 at the top, 0 at the bottom). A long stem means the model is confidently wrong there, and it is the raw material for the loss.

</Step>

<Step title="Why not squared error? Log-loss">

For a point with true label $y$ and predicted probability $p$, a natural score is how surprised the model is by the truth. **Log-loss** (binary cross-entropy) is

$$\ell(p, y) = -\big[\,y \ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p)\,\big].$$

For a class 1 point this is $-\ln p$. If the model said $p = 0.9$, the loss is $0.105$. If it said $p = 0.1$, the loss is $2.30$. If it said $p = 0.01$, it is $4.61$. Confident and wrong is punished heavily, while confident and right costs almost nothing. The average of $\ell$ over all points is the number in the readout. A model that always says $0.5$ scores $\ln 2 = 0.693$; anything lower beats guessing.

Watch the curve steepen while $(w, b)$ moves along the dashed line on the map, scaled from 0.1 to 3 times the best fit (the boundary stays put). Too flat, the loss is 0.634 because nothing is decided. At the best fit it is 0.452. Beyond that the curve becomes overconfident on the overlapping points, and at three times the fit the loss climbs back to 0.651. Only the middle setting is the right amount of confidence for this data.

<LogisticFit client:visible mode="steepen" title="Sharpening the curve: log-loss has a sweet spot" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Suppose each label is a coin flip with $P(y = 1) = p_i$. The likelihood of the data is $\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$. Taking $-\frac{1}{n}\ln$ of it gives exactly the mean log-loss, so minimizing log-loss is maximum likelihood estimation. With squared error, a sigmoid model has flat regions where the curve saturates, which makes learning stall; log-loss has no such plateau, and it is convex in $(w, b)$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Training: the gradient is prediction minus truth">

Log-loss has a remarkably tidy gradient. Using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, the sigmoid's slope cancels against the log, leaving

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i)\,x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i).$$

Each point pushes with a force equal to its error $p_i - y_i$, the same stem you see in the figure. A class 1 point with a low $p$ pulls the curve up towards it; a class 0 point with a high $p$ pushes it down.

Start at $w = 0.1$, $b = -2.2$, where the log-loss is 1.173. The gradient is $(-0.304, -0.403)$. With a step size of $0.5$ the first update gives $w = 0.252$, $b = -2.00$, and the loss falls to 1.050. After 10 steps it is 0.558 and after 40 it is 0.453, within 0.001 of the minimum $0.452$ at $w = 1.26$, $b = 0.44$. Because log-loss is convex, every start ends at the same place. See [gradient descent](/learn/foundations/gradient-descent/) for the update rule.

This figure shows the gradient itself. The magenta arrow on the map is the next step, $-0.5\,\nabla L$, drawn from the dot. The readout writes out both partial derivatives and where the step lands. Watch the arrow shrink as the dot nears the diamond: near the minimum the errors $p_i - y_i$ mostly cancel, so the gradient fades. Then click anywhere on the map to place your own starting guess and press Play. Training runs from your guess and heads for the same diamond, because there is only one bottom. From a far corner, such as large $w$ and large $b$, 40 steps are not enough: the valley floor there is nearly flat, so the arrow is tiny and progress is slow. That is a real weakness of plain gradient descent, and Day 6's optimizers are built to fix it.

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

def train(x, y, lr=0.5, steps=40):
    w, b = 0.1, -2.2
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(w * x + b)
        w -= lr * np.mean((p - y) * x)
        b -= lr * np.mean(p - y)
    return w, b
```

</details>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** logistic regression is a regression that predicts numbers. **Actually** it is a classifier: the "regression" is the linear score inside, and the output is a probability.
- **You might think** a probability of 0.9 means the model is 90% accurate. **Actually** it says that among inputs like this one, about 90% should be class 1. Whether that holds is a separate question (calibration).
- **You might think** the fit should get every point right. **Actually** the classes overlap here, so the best curve still misclassifies some points (11 of 16 are correct at the optimum). Forcing them all right only makes the curve overconfident.
- **You might think** the sigmoid makes the model non-linear in the input. **Actually** the boundary $wx + b = 0$ is still a straight cut; the sigmoid only shapes how confidence grows.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>What is $\sigma(0)$, and what does it mean for a point to sit at score 0?</summary>

$\sigma(0) = 0.5$. The model is undecided: the point is exactly on the decision boundary $x = -b/w$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A class 1 point gets $p = 0.2$. What is its log-loss?</summary>

$-\ln 0.2 = 1.61$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does scaling both $w$ and $b$ up by the same factor do to the curve?</summary>

The boundary $-b/w$ stays put and the curve gets steeper around it, so the model becomes more confident on both sides.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is the gradient for a point just $(p - y)\,x$?</summary>

The sigmoid's derivative $p(1 - p)$ cancels with the $1/p$ and $1/(1 - p)$ from differentiating the logarithm, leaving the error $p - y$ times the input.

</details>
