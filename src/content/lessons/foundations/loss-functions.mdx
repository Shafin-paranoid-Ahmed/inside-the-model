<Step title="How wrong is this line?">

You can look at a line through a cloud of points and say "that is a bad fit". A computer cannot look. To improve a model automatically, we first need a *number* that says how badly it is doing, so that "better" can mean "the number went down".

That number is called the **loss**. Start with a single prediction. Each vertical segment in the figure is the gap between what a point really is and what the line predicted for it. Blue segments are points above the line, orange are points below.

Watch the timeline tilt the line away from the best fit and back. Long segments mean a bad fit, and they shrink as the line settles. The readout lists every gap with its sign. You can also grab either round handle and drag the line yourself. There is no score yet. Turning these eight gaps into one number is the next step.

<LossPlayground client:visible view="residuals" title="Residuals: the gap between each point and the line" />

</Step>

<Step title="Error of one prediction: the residual">

For one example with true label $y$ and prediction $\hat y$, the **residual** is the signed gap:

$$r = y - \hat y.$$

It is positive when the model predicted too low (the point is above the line) and negative when it predicted too high. The sign tells you which way you missed. For a *score* of wrongness we usually do not care about direction, only about size, so we throw the sign away in one of two ways.

The first is the **absolute error**, $|r|$. The second is the **squared error**, $r^2$. Both are zero for a perfect prediction and grow as the miss grows, but they grow differently: a residual of 2 costs 2 under the absolute error and 4 under the squared error, while a residual of 10 costs 10 versus 100. Squaring punishes large misses much more than small ones.

</Step>

<Step title="Mean squared error, with actual squares">

To score a whole dataset, average the per-example losses. Averaging the squared errors gives the **mean squared error** (MSE); averaging the absolute errors gives the **mean absolute error** (MAE).

The figure draws each squared error as a real square, with side length equal to the residual. Its area is $r^2$. The MSE is the average area of these squares, so a line is a good fit when the squares are small. Switch to "Absolute (MAE)" under the figure and the squares disappear, leaving bars whose *length* is the error. The readout shows both scores for the current line. As the line tilts, watch the big misses: their squares grow much faster than their bars. For the best line here (slope 0.6, intercept 1.0) the MSE is 0.135 and the MAE is 0.35.

<LossPlayground client:visible view="squares" title="MSE: the average area of the squares" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

With $n$ examples $(x_i, y_i)$ and a model $\hat y_i = w x_i + b$:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat y_i|.$$

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def mse(y, y_hat): return np.mean((y - y_hat) ** 2)
def mae(y, y_hat): return np.mean(np.abs(y - y_hat))
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Loss as a function of the knobs">

Here is the idea that the whole rest of the course is built on. The data is fixed. So the loss depends only on the model's knobs: change $w$ and $b$ and the loss changes. The loss is a *function of the parameters*, written $L(w, b)$.

This figure adds what the previous ones lacked: a second chart, on the right. It does not show the data. It shows the MSE for *every* slope $w$ at once, with $b$ held fixed. Each point on that curve is one whole line on the left, scored. The accent dot is the current line. The diamond marks the lowest point, the best $w$ for this $b$. As the timeline tilts the line, the dot rides up and down the bowl. Drag the line's handles yourself and the dot follows. The bottom of the bowl is where the line fits best.

<LossPlayground client:visible view="curve" title="The loss as a function of the slope" />

Training will mean exactly this: find the knob values at the bottom of the curve. The next two lessons show how to find them without looking at a plot.

</Step>

<Step title="Why squared error, and what an outlier does to it">

Why square rather than take absolute values? Squared error is smooth, so it has a slope everywhere (which gradient descent will need). It also has a clean closed-form minimum for a line, which you will meet in the linear-regression lesson. And it corresponds to a specific statistical assumption: if the noise in the labels is Gaussian, minimizing squared error is the maximum-likelihood fit.

The price is sensitivity to **outliers**, points that do not follow the pattern. Watch the timeline slide one point far below the trend while the line stays exactly where it was. The MSE jumps from 0.135 to 3.48, about 26 times larger. The MAE goes from 0.35 to 0.92, about 2.6 times larger. Because the squares of big misses are huge, one bad point can dominate the whole MSE.

That also changes where the bottom of the curve is. With the intercept held at 1.0, the MSE curve's best slope moves from 0.58 to 0.24 when the outlier appears: the line is dragged toward the outlier. The MAE curve's best slope stays at 0.51. Switch the loss to "Absolute (MAE)" and replay: the bowl on the right barely moves.

<LossPlayground client:visible view="outlier" title="One outlier: MSE panics, MAE barely notices" />

Neither is "right". If big misses really are much worse than small ones, use squared error. If a few wild points are noise you would rather ignore, use absolute error. (A popular compromise, the Huber loss, is squared for small misses and absolute for large ones.)

</Step>

<Step title="Classification: the model answers with a probability">

So far the label was a number, like a price. Often it is a yes-or-no answer instead: spam or not spam, tumor or no tumor. We write the two answers as label 1 and label 0.

Residuals stop making sense here. The labels 0 and 1 are names, not amounts, so "the prediction was 0.3 away" means nothing. A classifier does something more useful than guess a label. It says **how sure** it is: "I think there is a 90% chance this is label 1." The loss then asks one question: *how much probability did you give to the right answer?*

In the figure below, each dot is one example with one feature $x$. Filled dots sit on the top line (label 1); open dots sit on the bottom line (label 0). The teal S-shaped curve is the model. Its height above any $x$ is the probability it gives to label 1 there. (The S-shape comes from the **sigmoid** function $\sigma$, which squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. You only need the picture for now. Logistic regression on Day 2 builds it properly.)

So for every dot you can read off how much probability it got:

- a **label-1** dot gets $p$, the height of the curve above it;
- a **label-0** dot gets $1 - p$, the space *above* the curve.

The red bar on each dot runs from the dot to the curve. Its length is the probability the model *withheld* from the right answer. A short bar means the model was nearly sure and right.

</Step>

<Step title="Log loss: pay for the probability you withheld">

Now turn "probability given to the right answer", call it $p$, into a cost. The rule is

$$\ell = -\ln p.$$

That is all the **log loss** (also called cross-entropy) is. Some values:

| Probability given to the right answer | 0.99 | 0.9 | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.01 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loss $-\ln p$ | 0.01 | 0.11 | 0.69 | 2.30 | 4.61 |

Read the table from left to right. Being sure and right costs almost nothing. A shrug (0.5) costs 0.69. Being sure and **wrong** is very expensive: the cost keeps climbing toward infinity as $p$ heads to 0. A model trained on this loss learns never to be confidently wrong.

The chart on the right of the figure is this rule as a curve. Every dot from the left panel sits on it, at the probability it was given. Dots on the right of that chart are cheap. Dots near the left wall are expensive. The dashed line marks a shrug: probability 0.5, cost 0.693.

Watch the timeline sharpen the S-curve. Eight dots slide to the cheap right-hand end. Two dots are on the wrong side of the curve (ringed in red): the label-1 dot at $x = -0.5$ and the label-0 dot at $x = 0.3$. As the model grows more confident, those two climb the wall. When the curve is sharpest, the average log loss is 0.36, and those two points account for 95% of it. You can drag the round handle to shift the curve sideways, or use the sliders.

<LossPlayground client:visible view="classify" title="Log loss: confident mistakes cost the most" />

The training loss is the average of $-\ln p$ over all examples. Guessing 0.5 for everything scores exactly $\ln 2 = 0.693$, so any useful classifier must beat that.

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** the loss is a measure of accuracy. **Actually** the loss is a smooth number we can minimize; accuracy just counts right answers. A model can improve its loss without changing a single answer.
- **You might think** there is one correct loss. **Actually** the loss is a design choice that encodes what mistakes matter. Squared and absolute error disagree exactly when outliers are present.
- **You might think** the loss is a property of the model alone. **Actually** it is a property of the model *and* the data: change either and the number changes.
- **You might think** a lower loss on the training data always means a better model. **Actually** a model can fit the training points beautifully and still fail on new ones. Later lessons on generalization return to this.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>A model predicts 7 and the true value is 10. What are the residual, the absolute error and the squared error?</summary>

Residual $10 - 7 = 3$, absolute error 3, squared error 9.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Residuals are 1, -2 and 3. What is the MSE? The MAE?</summary>

MSE $= (1 + 4 + 9)/3 = 4.67$. MAE $= (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 2$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does an outlier hurt MSE more than MAE?</summary>

Squaring makes a big residual count far more than a small one (10 counts as 100 instead of 10), so one large miss dominates the average.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A classifier gives probability 0.9 to the correct class. Is its log loss bigger or smaller than 0.693?</summary>

Smaller: $-\ln 0.9 = 0.105$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does "loss as a function of the parameters" mean?</summary>

With the data fixed, each choice of knob values gives one loss value, so the loss is a function $L(w, b)$ of the knobs.

</details>
