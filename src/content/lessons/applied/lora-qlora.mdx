A trained model already does much of what we need. Can we teach it a new task by learning a small correction? We will follow one linear layer, with 32 inputs and 32 outputs, from an unchanged base through adaptation, merging, and quantization. The large-model memory calculator is separate from this layer's training experiment.

<Step title="What full fine-tuning costs">

A **parameter** is one stored weight. Full fine-tuning changes every parameter. Training also keeps its **gradient** (which way to change it) and the optimizer's running information for choosing updates.

In the mixed-precision Adam arrangement described in [ZeRO, section 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.02054v3), each parameter needs 2 bytes for its working weight, 2 for its gradient, and 12 for a higher-precision weight copy and two optimizer statistics. That is $2+2+12=16$ bytes. Seven billion parameters therefore require $7\times10^9\times16=112\times10^9$ bytes, or **112 GB of model states**, before activations and temporary buffers.

Change the calculator to 70B: multiplying the parameter count by ten makes the total **1,120 GB**. Freezing most weights avoids most of the gradient and optimizer storage.

</Step>

<Step title="Learn a small correction: W + 2BA">

Our layer starts with a weight matrix $W$: 32 rows by 32 columns, or $32\times32=1,024$ weights. **LoRA** keeps $W$ frozen and trains two smaller matrices instead. At rank $r=2$, $A$ compresses 32 input values into 2 values; $B$ expands those 2 back into 32 corrections. Together these matrices are the **adapter**.

The adapted layer is $W'=W+sBA$. The multiplier $s=\alpha/r$ controls the update's scale; here we choose $\alpha=2r$, so $s=2$ at every rank. At rank 2, the number of trainable values is $32\times2+2\times32=64+64=128$, just $128/1,024=12.5\%$ of the full matrix. The update's rank is **at most** 2: its output changes combine at most two independent directions.

Look at the first-entry calculation below the heat map. A matrix product is a sum of products: multiply the first row of $B$ by the first column of $A$, add those contributions, multiply by 2, then add the original weight. Changing rank changes how many terms are available.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="lowrank" r={2} title="Two small matrices make a full-size correction" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For rank 2, the first entry is

$$
(BA)_{11}=B_{11}A_{11}+B_{12}A_{21},\qquad
W'_{11}=W_{11}+2(B_{11}A_{11}+B_{12}A_{21}).
$$

The figure substitutes the trained entries into this exact expression. The full forward pass is $h=Wx+2B(Ax)$, where $x$ is the input vector and $h$ the output. Initialize $B$ to zero and $A$ to small random values: $BA=0$ initially, so $h=Wx$. Initializing both to zero would also make both matrices' initial gradients zero.

</details>
</Step>

<Step title="Train the adapter on the same task">

The target task asks our layer to change mainly along two directions, with a small additional change in the other directions. Each training example has a 32-number input and a desired 32-number output. We use **mean squared error (MSE)**: subtract the desired output from the prediction, square each error, then average across outputs and examples. Lower is better.

Full fine-tuning changes all 1,024 weights. LoRA changes only $A$ and $B$. Both use Adam for 300 updates; the learning rates are 0.01 for full fine-tuning and 0.02 for adapters. The chart reports expected MSE on fresh examples from the same task, so memorizing training examples cannot hide a poor fit.

At update 300, full fine-tuning reaches **0.103**. Rank 1 uses 64 parameters but stops at **0.403**; ranks 2, 4 and 8 use 128, 256 and 512 parameters and each round to **0.103**. Scrub to the end and compare rank 1 with rank 2: the second direction makes the substantial difference here.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="train" title="Same target, different numbers of trainable values" />

<details>
<summary>Show how the loss connects to the model</summary>

The target outputs are generated by a target matrix $W_*$ plus independent noise with standard deviation 0.3. Inputs have independent, zero-mean, unit-variance entries. For this specific distribution, fresh-example MSE equals

$$
\operatorname{MSE}=\frac{\sum_{i,j}(W'_{ij}-W_{*,ij})^2}{32}+0.3^2
=\frac{\text{sum of squared weight differences}}{32}+0.09.
$$

Thus even matching the target matrix perfectly leaves expected MSE 0.09 because of the noisy labels. This is the same squared-error objective as the [Day 1 loss lesson](/learn/foundations/loss-functions/), applied to 32 outputs rather than one.

</details>
</Step>

<Step title="When low rank is enough�and when it is not">

Why did two directions suffice? Inspect the change learned by full fine-tuning, $W_{\text{full}}-W$. **Singular values** measure the strength of its independent directions. Squaring those strengths and adding them measures the total squared size of the change.

For our original task, the strongest two directions account for **97.5%** of that squared size. Now switch to the full-rank task. Its desired change is spread across many directions: the top two account for only **22.7%**, and the rank-2 adapter's fresh MSE rises to **0.862**. The full model still reaches 0.103. A small adapter is a useful restriction only when it leaves enough freedom for the task.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="spectrum" title="Does the task need more than two directions?" />

[Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685) found low-rank updates effective on their language-model benchmarks. Our counterexample explains why that finding is not a promise that rank 2 works for every task.
</Step>

<Step title="Merge the correction into the base">

After training, the adapter no longer changes. We can multiply $B$ and $A$ once and add $2BA$ to $W$. This is **merging**: replace the two-path computation $Wx+2B(Ax)$ by one multiplication $(W+2BA)x$.

For the rank-4 adapter, the unmerged path needs $32\times32+4\times32+32\times4=1,024+128+128=1,280$ multiply-adds per input. The merged path needs $32\times32=1,024$. Scrub through the fold and compare the first outputs: they agree to the displayed digits, with a largest difference of about $9\times10^{-16}$ across all outputs. That residual is floating-point rounding.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="merge" r={4} title="Same output, one matrix multiplication" />

You can keep separate adapters for different tasks. The switch uses another trained adapter with the same base. Merging into a higher-precision matrix removes the adapter's extra products; merging and then requantizing would introduce additional rounding error.
</Step>

<Step title="Store the frozen base in four bits">

LoRA reduces what we train; **quantization** reduces what we store. Instead of storing each frozen weight with many possible values, store a code choosing from a small table. Four bits can select $2^4=16$ entries. **QLoRA** combines this compact base with higher-precision trainable adapters.

We divide our same base matrix into blocks of 64 weights. For each block, take its largest absolute weight as a **scale**. Divide a weight by that scale, choose the nearest table entry, and multiply by the scale to reconstruct an approximation. Follow the worked weight under the figure: every division, rounding choice, and multiplication is shown.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="quantize" title="One weight: divide, choose a level, multiply back" />

NF4 uses unevenly spaced levels, with more near zero, where normally distributed weights are concentrated. Switch to the symmetric integer scheme: its 15 levels correspond to integers -7 through 7, leaving one four-bit code unused. Across this base matrix, reconstructed-weight MSE is **0.000821 for NF4** and **0.000927 for symmetric int4**. These are errors in weights, not prediction losses.

A 32-bit scale shared by 64 weights adds $32/64=0.5$ bits per weight. Storage here is therefore $4+0.5=4.5$ bits, or $4.5/8=0.5625$ bytes per base weight. [QLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.14314v1) also quantizes the scales themselves, called double quantization; that further saving is not included in our calculator.

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
# block has 64 weights; levels contains the NF4 reconstruction values
scale = max(abs(block))
normalized = block / scale if scale else block * 0
codes = abs(normalized[:, None] - levels[None, :]).argmin(axis=1)
reconstructed = levels[codes] * scale
weight_mse = ((block - reconstructed) ** 2).mean()
```

</details>
</Step>

<Step title="Train on top of the quantized base">

Now return to the original two-direction task. Keep the reconstructed NF4 base fixed and train a rank-4 adapter on top of it. Its fresh-example loss falls from **1.077 without adaptation** to **0.119** after training. The same-rank adapter on the unquantized base reaches **0.103**. The adapter learns the task despite the rounded base, but it does not erase every consequence of quantization.

Scrub to update 300 and compare those two losses. The memory line is a separate large-model calculation: at 7B parameters, full fine-tuning uses **112.00 GB**, LoRA **14.27 GB**, and our quantized-base arrangement **4.21 GB** of model states. It assumes rank-16 adapters on four attention projections in each of 32 layers, each projection 4,096 wide; activations and temporary buffers are additional.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="qlora" r={4} title="A compact base with a trainable correction" />

<details>
<summary>Show the memory arithmetic</summary>

The adapter parameter count is $4\times32\times16\times(4096+4096)=16,777,216$. At 16 bytes per trainable parameter, its states occupy $268,435,456$ bytes, or about 0.268 GB. Add the frozen base: LoRA uses $7\times2+0.268=14.268$ GB; quantized LoRA uses $7\times0.5625+0.268=4.206$ GB. The calculator rounds these totals to two decimals.

</details>

The QLoRA paper reports fine-tuning a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU. That is a measured result of its complete system, including double quantization and paged optimizers, rather than a guarantee from our model-state arithmetic alone.
</Step>

<Step title="A small adapter still needs enough examples">

Fewer trainable parameters do not remove overfitting. Keep our rank-4 adapter and original task, but vary the number of training examples. With only **4 examples**, training MSE rounds to **0.000**, while fresh-example MSE is **1.272**�worse than the initial 1.040. Fitting those four examples did not teach the wider task.

Follow the timeline to **256 examples**: training MSE is **0.098** and fresh-example MSE **0.103**. The small gap now supports using the learned change on more examples from this distribution.

<LoRALab client:visible mode="data" r={4} title="Compare fit to the examples with fit to the task" />

For a new application, start by testing [prompting](/learn/applied/prompting/) for the desired behavior. Consider fine-tuning when you have representative examples and an evaluation set that can show improvement. For facts that change, [retrieval](/learn/applied/rag/) lets you update the information supplied to the model without retraining its weights.
</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **�LoRA makes the base model smaller.�** It keeps the base and reduces the trainable part. Quantization is the separate storage reduction.
- **�A rank-2 adapter always matches full fine-tuning.�** Our original task nearly allows it; the full-rank counterexample does not.
- **�Four-bit weights mean four-bit training everywhere.�** QLoRA stores the frozen base compactly and computes with reconstructed weights while training higher-precision adapters.
- **�Zero training loss proves the adapter learned the task.�** Four examples gave near-zero training loss and poor fresh-example loss.
- **�The memory estimate tells me which GPU will fit.�** Model states are only part of the allocation; activations and temporary working storage also matter.
</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>How many parameters does rank 8 add to a 4,096 � 4,096 matrix?</summary>

$8\times(4096+4096)=65,536$. The full matrix has $4096^2=16,777,216$, so the fraction is $65,536/16,777,216=1/256\approx0.39\%$.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Why initialize B to zero but A to random values?</summary>

Zero $B$ makes the initial correction zero, preserving the base output. Nonzero $A$ lets the gradient begin changing $B$; setting both to zero would stall both gradients.

</details>
<details>
<summary>A block scale is 0.8 and its normalized stored level is 0.25. What weight is reconstructed?</summary>

Multiply the level by the scale: $0.25\times0.8=0.20$. This recovers the quantized approximation, not necessarily the exact original weight.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Training loss is nearly zero on four examples. Should you conclude the adaptation succeeded?</summary>

No. Evaluate on fresh examples. In our run their MSE was 1.272, despite fitting the training examples almost perfectly.

</details>
</Step>
