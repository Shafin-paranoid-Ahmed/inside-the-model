What if the model could critique itself against a written list of principles, so that people write rules once instead of judging thousands of answers?

<Step title="A constitution: principles written down">

In [RLHF](/learn/alignment/rlhf/), people compared pairs of answers, and their taste, including their liking for long answers, ended up inside the reward model without anyone writing it down. **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., 2022) takes the opposite route: write the standards down as a short list of plain-language principles, the **constitution**, and let an AI apply them. Anyone can read the list and argue with it.

As in the earlier lessons, a real model writes its response token by token. Here the model picks one of eight candidate responses to each prompt, so every number below can be computed exactly. The AI that applies the principles is also a stand-in: in the paper a language model reads them, while here a small **rule checker** tests each response against each principle, so you can see every judgement.

The figure checks all eight candidates for "What is 7 + 5?" against four principles. A cross means the response breaks that principle; the last column counts them. The padded answer breaks the flattery rule, the refusal breaks two principles at once (it does not answer, and it refuses a harmless request), and only three candidates break nothing: terse correct, clear correct and hedged correct. Those are the responses the constitution endorses.

<Step title="Critique and revision">

Checking responses is half the job. The other half is fixing them. In the **critique and revision** loop the model takes one of its own responses, picks a principle at random, and asks whether the response breaks it (the critique). If it does, the model rewrites the response to fix that problem (the revision). The loop repeats for several rounds, each time with a fresh random principle, and each rewrite is meant to change as little as possible besides the fix.

A model that is already tidy has little to fix, so here the starting responses come from the base model, the one that just continues the list of questions. The figure follows three of them through four rounds. In the first, on "Spell 'cat' backwards.", round 1 picks "Be accurate" and the response, a continuation of the list, has nothing wrong with accuracy, so it is kept. Round 2 picks "Answer the question that was asked": the model replaces the list with a terse answer, which turns out to be wrong. Round 3 picks "Be accurate", and the wrong answer becomes the right one. Round 4 finds nothing left to fix. Fixing one principle can break another, which is why several rounds are needed.

Across all 64 starting responses the loop lowers the principles broken per response from 1.16 to 0.77, and in 206 of the 256 rounds nothing needed fixing. Some starts are not finished after four rounds, since a random principle only fixes what it happens to look at.

```python
for _ in range(rounds):

    critique = model(f"Does this break '{principle}'? {response}")

```

<Step title="Fine-tuning on the revisions">

The critique and revision loop is slow, since it needs several model calls per answer. The fix is to run it once to build a dataset, then use ordinary supervised fine-tuning, as in the [SFT lesson](/learn/alignment/sft/): train the model to produce the revised responses directly. This is the first stage of Constitutional AI, called SL-CAI.

Here the SFT model writes 64 responses and the loop revises them; 12 of the 64 broke a principle and 7 still do after revision. The model is then fine-tuned for 150 steps on the revised set. Watch the bars: the outline marks each response's chance before fine-tuning. The terse wrong answer starts at 0.14 and falls to 0.09, and the principles broken per response drop from 0.207 to 0.110. The reward that stands for what the person really wanted, the **gold reward** (real projects never see it, but it is how this figure scores the result), rises from 2.27 to 2.51, although the training never used it.

<Step title="AI feedback in place of human labels">

The second stage replaces the human labeler of RLHF. The AI is shown two responses to the same prompt and asked which one follows the constitution better. Its choices form a preference dataset exactly like the human one, and everything after that (reward model, PPO) is unchanged. Training from AI preference labels is **RLAIF**, reinforcement learning from AI feedback.

Here the judge prefers the response that breaks fewer principles, and breaks ties with a coin flip when both break the same number. The figure uses the same 200 pairs as the first RLHF figure and shows what the AI judge picked next to what the simulated human picked. The AI agrees with the gold ranking on 71% of the pairs and the human labeler on 69%, and the two labelers agree with each other on 71%, so they disagree on 58 pairs. Click a ringed square to see one. In 53 of those 58, both responses break the same number of principles and the coin decided, usually between a clear and a hedged correct answer, because no principle prefers either. In the other 5, such as pair 34 ("What colour do you get by mixing blue and yellow?"), the status line names the deciding principle: the AI avoided a wrong answer that the human had picked.

<Step title="RLAIF end to end">

Fit a reward model to the AI labels and run the same PPO loop as in RLHF, and you get a policy shaped by the constitution rather than by the labelers' habits. The clearest difference shows when the KL penalty is removed. In the [RLHF lesson](/learn/alignment/rlhf/) the human-label reward model rewarded length, and with the penalty at $\beta = 0$ the policy found the rambling wrong answer and its gold reward collapsed. The AI labels never rewarded length, because no principle mentions it.

The faint line in the figure is that human-label run, ending at a gold reward of $-0.44$. The dashed line is the run trained on AI labels with the same settings. It peaks early, at 2.47 on update 2, and ends at 2.17, with a chance of the rambling answer of 0.00 (the proxy reward reaches 4.78 and the KL to the SFT model 1.59). The policy has settled on the hedged correct answer, "I think it might be 12, but I'm not completely sure", on the first prompt with probability 0.99. It avoided the exploit that length-loving labelers invited, but not a different one: none of the four principles penalises hedging, so the reward model scores it as safe.

<Step title="Who writes the principles?">

The behaviour now follows from the text of the constitution, so a careless principle shows up directly in the model. That is an advantage, since you can read what the model was told to value and change it. It is also a responsibility: someone has to decide which principles go in and what to do when two of them conflict. Anthropic's published constitution, for instance, drew on sources such as the UN Declaration of Human Rights and other labs' published principles, and its authors call it neither finalized nor the best it can be.

Each toggle below is a principle, and every change retrains the whole pipeline on the new rules: labels, reward model and PPO at $\beta = 0.1$. With the four default principles the policy ends refusing or hedging with probability 0.358 and gold reward 2.61, a little below the 2.81 that human labels reached at the same $\beta$ in the RLHF lesson, because hedging is unpenalised. Switch on "Avoid anything that could be wrong", which sounds prudent, and the judge now prefers any hedged answer: the chance of refusing or hedging goes to 0.999 and gold falls to 2.17. Switch off "No flattery" instead and the policy settles on padded answers, with gold 1.15. Each principle you add or remove has a consequence you can see in the bars.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think Constitutional AI removes humans.** People write the principles, choose the prompts and evaluate the result. In Bai et al. the harmlessness labels came from an AI, but the helpfulness labels were still human.

**You might think the model obeys the constitution like code.** The principles shape the training data and the labels. The behaviour is learned, so it is imperfect: in the figures above, four rounds of revision left some responses still breaking a principle.

**You might think AI labels are unbiased.** They carry the biases of the principles and of the judge. Here the judge had no length bias but also no objection to hedging, and the policy hedged. Real language-model judges have documented biases of their own, such as favouring longer answers or the first answer shown (Zheng et al., 2023).

**You might think more principles are always safer.** The over-cautious principle produced a policy that hedged on nearly every answer.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>What are the two training stages of Constitutional AI?</summary>

First, supervised learning: the model critiques and revises its own responses, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. Second, reinforcement learning from AI feedback: an AI labels preference pairs using the constitution, a reward model is trained on them, and the policy is optimised against it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why sample one principle per round instead of checking all of them at once?</summary>

It keeps each critique focused, and over many rounds and many responses all the principles get applied. This is how the paper's procedure works. Note the cost shown in the figure: a single random principle can miss what is wrong, so a response may need several rounds.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A response pads a correct answer with flattery. Which principle catches it, and what is the revision?</summary>

"Do not pad the answer with flattery". The revision moves to the clear correct answer, which keeps the answer and drops the padding.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why did RLAIF in the figure not reward long answers?</summary>

The labels came from rules about answering, accuracy, flattery and refusing, and none mentions length. The reward model had no length signal to learn, so there was nothing for the policy to exploit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Give one risk of letting an AI judge label its own training data.</summary>

Its blind spots become the model's. Here the judge never penalised hedging, so the trained policy hedged. A judge can also share its biases with the model it trains, so errors reinforce rather than cancel.

</details>

</Step>
