How can an agent reuse a good batch without chasing it too far?

<Step title="An advantage compares an action with the usual outcome">

After the agent collects a batch of experience, PPO has to decide which of the actions it took were good. Raw rewards are a poor judge: a step that earns $-0.04$ near the goal is better than one that earns $-0.04$ far from it. So the critic (the value estimate $V$ from the [policy gradients lesson](/learn/alignment/policy-gradients/)) supplies a baseline. The **advantage** of an action is how much better the outcome was than the critic expected from that state. Positive means "do this more", negative means "do this less".

The simplest advantage is the one-step **TD error**, $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$: the reward plus the discounted value of where you landed, minus the value you expected. It only looks one step ahead, though, and a critic that is still learning can be badly wrong. **Generalized advantage estimation (GAE)** adds up the later errors too, each shrunk by $(\gamma\lambda)^l$. The knob $\lambda$ decides how far to look: $\lambda = 0$ keeps only $\delta_t$, and values near 1 look to the end of the episode.

Watch the table fill in one episode of six steps. In step 1 the agent moves up from $(1,1)$ and its own error is only $+0.043$, because the cell it reached was worth almost exactly what the critic expected. Then the later errors are added to step 1. The last step reaches the +1 goal and its error is $+0.875$; discounted by $(0.9 \times 0.95)^5$ it adds $+0.400$ to step 1. With $\lambda = 0.95$ the advantage of step 1 is $+0.493$, more than ten times its one-step error.

<GridWorldLab client:visible mode="ppo-advantage" gamma={0.9} lambda={0.95} batch={64} seed={29} title="One episode: each step's error, then the advantage of step 1" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad \hat A_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}.$$

At a terminal move $V(s_{t+1})$ counts as 0 and the sum stops. If an episode is cut off by a time limit instead, $V(s_{t+1})$ is still used in $\delta_t$ (the episode could have continued) but the sum stops. Computed backwards, $\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda \hat A_{t+1}$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
adv, nxt = np.zeros(n), 0.0
for t in reversed(range(n)):
    live = 0.0 if terminated[t] else 1.0
    delta = r[t] + gamma * live * v_next[t] - v[t]
    nxt = delta + gamma * lam * live * (0.0 if truncated[t] else 1.0) * nxt
    adv[t] = nxt
returns = adv + v          # the critic's regression target
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Keep the policy that collected the batch">

PPO will reuse each batch for several update steps, so the policy changes while the batch stays the same. To measure how much it has changed, PPO saves the probability of every action at the moment it was chosen, $\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)$. After each update it divides the current probability by that saved one:

$$\rho = \frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}.$$

A ratio of 1 means "unchanged", above 1 means the action became more likely, below 1 less likely. The saved probabilities are frozen for the whole batch; the next batch will save new ones.

The figure shows 17 visits to the start cell $(1,1)$. The dashed outlines are the saved probabilities (up 0.33, right 0.16, down 0.37, left 0.14) and the filled bars are the current ones. Each chip is one visit, blue for a positive advantage and orange for a negative one; click a chip to follow it. The first chip is an "up" with advantage $+0.49$: after 16 updates its probability has grown from 0.325 to 0.355, a ratio of 1.092. Now click the tenth chip, a "right" with advantage $-0.24$: its ratio falls to 0.746.

<GridWorldLab client:visible mode="ppo-ratio" focus="1,1" batch={64} epochs={4} seed={29} title="Saved probabilities against current ones, in the start cell" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

In practice the ratio is computed from log-probabilities, which are numerically safer: $\rho = \exp(\log\pi_{\text{new}} - \log\pi_{\text{old}})$. At the start of every batch $\pi_{\text{new}} = \pi_{\text{old}}$, so $\rho = 1$ exactly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
# at collection time
logp_old = policy.log_prob(a, s).detach()   # saved, never recomputed
# in every update step
ratio = torch.exp(policy.log_prob(a, s) - logp_old)
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Clip the incentive to increase a good action">

If we simply maximised $\rho A$ over a batch, one lucky sample could push the policy a long way, because nothing stops the ratio from growing. PPO's fix is to **clip** the ratio inside the objective: choose a small $\varepsilon$ (0.2 here) and use

$$\min\bigl(\rho A,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho,\ 1-\varepsilon,\ 1+\varepsilon)\,A\bigr).$$

The shaded **band** is $1-\varepsilon$ to $1+\varepsilon$. Take a good action, $A = +1$. Inside the band the objective is just $\rho A$ and its slope is $A$, so the update keeps raising the action's probability. Above $1.2$ the clipped term is the smaller one and the objective goes flat: the slope is 0, and there is no more reward for making the action even more likely. Below the band the plain $\rho A$ is smaller, so lowering a good action's probability still costs objective.

The cursor pauses at two ratios. At $\rho = 1.2$ the objective is $1.2$, and at $\rho = 1.5$ it is still $1.2$ while the dashed unclipped line has reached $1.5$.

<PPOLab client:visible mode="clip-positive" advantage={1} epsilon={0.2} ratios="0.5,1.6" title="Clipped objective for an advantage of +1" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For $A > 0$ the objective is $\rho A$ for $\rho \le 1+\varepsilon$ and $(1+\varepsilon)A$ above, so it has slope $A$ and then slope $0$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Negative advantages clip in the other direction">

For a bad action, $A = -1$, the same formula flips sides. The minimum now picks the plateau below the band: once the ratio drops under $0.8$ the objective is stuck at $-0.8$ and there is no more reward for making the bad action rarer. Above the band nothing is clipped: the objective $\rho A$ keeps falling as the action becomes more likely, so the update keeps pushing it back down.

That is why "outside the band" does not mean "zero gradient". Which side is flat depends on the sign of the advantage. Read the cursor at its two rests. At $\rho = 0.6$ the objective is $-0.8$ with slope 0. At $\rho = 1.4$ it is $-1.4$ and the slope is still $-1$, so a bad action that has become too likely is still penalised in full.

<PPOLab client:visible mode="clip-negative" advantage={-1} epsilon={0.2} ratios="0.5,1.6" title="Clipped objective for an advantage of −1" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For $A < 0$, $\rho A \ge (1-\varepsilon)A$ exactly when $\rho \le 1-\varepsilon$, so below the band the minimum is the constant $(1-\varepsilon)A$. Above the band $\rho A$ is the smaller one, so the slope stays $A$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="A few epochs reuse one batch">

Collecting experience is the expensive part, so PPO squeezes several updates out of each batch. An **epoch** is one pass over the batch, split into shuffled **mini-batches** (here 16 samples each); PPO does four epochs, which is 16 updates for 64 samples. Throughout, the saved probabilities, the advantages and the critic's targets stay frozen. The critic is trained separately, on its own loss.

In this figure each square is one step of the batch, coloured by the sign of its advantage (32 positive in blue, 32 negative in orange). The outlined squares are the mini-batch being used. The histogram counts how many ratios sit at each value: teal bars are samples still pushing, grey bars are flat samples (their ratio is outside the band on the side that earns nothing more). Early on every ratio is inside the band. After 7 updates the first ratio leaves it. At the end, 31% of the ratios are outside the band and 27% of the samples are flat, so 3 samples are outside the band but still pushing. Look for the ring in the grid: a sample like that has a positive advantage with a ratio below 0.8, or a negative advantage with a ratio above 1.2. The mean clipped objective rises from 0.124 to 0.145.

<GridWorldLab client:visible mode="ppo-epochs" batch={64} minibatch={16} epochs={4} epsilon={0.2} lr={0.03} seed={31} title="Sixteen updates on one frozen batch" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
batch = collect(policy, 64)                 # states, actions, rewards
logp_old, adv, ret = freeze(batch, critic)  # computed once
for epoch in range(4):
    for idx in shuffled_minibatches(64, size=16):
        ratio = (policy.log_prob(a[idx], s[idx]) - logp_old[idx]).exp()
        surr = torch.min(ratio * adv[idx], ratio.clamp(0.8, 1.2) * adv[idx])
        policy_loss = -surr.mean()
        value_loss = 0.5 * (critic(s[idx]) - ret[idx]).pow(2).mean()
        step(policy_loss, value_loss)       # separate optimisers
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Clipping encourages small moves but does not enforce a wall">

It is tempting to read the clip as a fence that keeps the ratio between $1-\varepsilon$ and $1+\varepsilon$. It is not. The clip only removes the incentive from one sample's term. The policy parameters are shared by many samples, and the optimizer's momentum can carry them on, so a ratio whose own gradient is zero can still keep moving. The only way to know how far the policy really moved is to measure it. The standard measure is the **KL divergence** from the old policy to the new one, averaged over the states in the batch: $0$ means the policy is unchanged, and larger means further away.

Here the same frozen batch and starting policy are trained three times, with $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $0.2$ and $0.4$. Each strip shows all 64 ratios. After 16 updates the ratios span 0.73 to 1.32 for $\varepsilon = 0.1$, 0.72 to 1.44 for $\varepsilon = 0.2$ and 0.71 to 1.58 for $\varepsilon = 0.4$. So even the narrowest band, which ends at 1.1, ends up with a ratio of 1.32. The width of the band does matter, though: the KL ends at 0.0114, 0.0176 and 0.0237, and 59%, 31% and 14% of the ratios are outside their bands. In this run, a smaller $\varepsilon$ means less drift, but never zero drift and never a hard limit.

<PPOLab client:visible mode="drift" epsilons="0.1,0.2,0.4" epochs={4} batch={64} lr={0.03} seed={31} title="Same batch, three clip widths" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

$$\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{new}}) = \sum_a \pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)\,\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{new}}(a \mid s)},$$

averaged over the states in the batch. This compares the new policy with the one that collected the batch. RLHF's KL penalty compares the policy with a fixed reference model, a different quantity with a different job. Some implementations also stop the epochs early once a measured KL passes a target.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Collect again after updating">

After its few epochs, PPO throws the batch away. It collects a fresh one with the updated policy, saves new old probabilities (so every ratio is 1 again), recomputes the advantages with the updated critic, and repeats. Unlike DQN's replay buffer in the [deep RL lesson](/learn/alignment/deep-rl/), nothing from earlier iterations is reused.

Watch 100 iterations on the grid. The arrows are the policy's action probabilities. The value of the start cell under the current policy begins at $-0.410$ with the uniform policy, reaches $+0.264$ after 20 iterations, $+0.330$ after 50 and $+0.341$ after 100, against $0.351$ for the best possible policy. Most of the gain comes early. The critic's error against the true values falls from 0.443 to 0.227 at iteration 20 and 0.157 at iteration 100. This is one run (seed 37). Other seeds reach 0.18 to 0.29 at iteration 20 and 0.340 to 0.343 at iteration 100, so the end result is steady and the early speed varies.

    for epoch in range(4):

            update_critic(idx, ret)
    # the batch is dropped; the next one starts again at ratio 1

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**"PPO keeps every probability ratio inside the band."** It clips a term of the objective, not the ratio. In the drift figure, the narrowest band (up to 1.1) still ends with a ratio of 1.32.

**"Every sample outside the band stops contributing."** Only the favourable side is flat, and the advantage's sign decides which side that is. A bad action whose ratio is 1.4 is still pushed down at full strength.

**"The old policy is the one from before training began."** It is the policy that collected the current batch, and it is replaced when the next batch is collected.

**"PPO replays old experience like DQN."** It reuses the current batch for a few epochs and then discards it.

**"Clipping and the RLHF reference penalty are the same thing."** Clipping compares with the policy that collected the batch. The RLHF penalty compares with a fixed reference model.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>1. The old probability of an action is 0.25 and the new one is 0.35. What is the ratio?</summary>

$0.35 / 0.25 = 1.4$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. For $A = 1$ and $\varepsilon = 0.2$, what is the objective at ratios 1.1 and 1.5?</summary>

At 1.1 the ratio is inside the band, so the objective is $1.1$. At 1.5 it is clipped to $1.2$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. For $A = -1$, which side of the band is flat, and why does a ratio of 1.4 still get a penalty?</summary>

The side below $1-\varepsilon$ is flat. At 1.4 the minimum of $-1.4$ and $-1.2$ is $-1.4$, the unclipped term, so the gradient is still $-1$ and the action is pushed back down.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Which quantities stay fixed while the four epochs optimise one batch?</summary>

The batch itself, the saved old log-probabilities, the advantages and the critic's regression targets.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Can clipping guarantee a small KL to the original SFT model?</summary>

No. Clipping has no hard bound even on the KL to the collection policy (the drift figure shows ratios far outside the band). And it compares with the policy that collected the current batch, which changes every iteration, so it says nothing about distance from a fixed SFT reference. That needs a separate penalty.

</details>
