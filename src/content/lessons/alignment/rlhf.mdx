People find it easier to rank two answers than to write a perfect one.

<Step title="Comparing two answers">

Instead of writing ideal responses, labelers are shown two responses to the same prompt and pick the one they prefer, which is faster and works even when they could not write the better answer themselves.

<Step title="The Bradley-Terry reward model">

A reward model gives every response a score, trained so that the probability of preferring one response over another is the sigmoid of the difference of their scores.

{/*
IDEA: Bradley-Terry: P(y_w preferred to y_l) = sigma(r(y_w) - r(y_l)); train by logistic regression on pairs; scores are only defined up to a constant.
WIDGET: PreferenceLab mode="reward-model" labels="human" pairs=200 steps=200. Feature-weight bars of the reward model next to the gold weights; pairwise accuracy on held-out pairs; the loss curve.
MOTION over t: RM weights grow from 0 per training step; loss draws; accuracy counts.
TRY: compare the reward model's weight on "length" with the gold weight.
NUMBERS: held-out pairwise accuracy; RM vs gold weight on correct and on length.
SHOW THE MATH: BT likelihood and loss -log sigma(r_w - r_l). SHOW THE CODE: reward-model loss in PyTorch.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="What the reward model learned">

The reward model learns whatever predicted the labels, including the labelers' biases, and it is only checked on the kinds of responses it saw.

{/*

WIDGET: PreferenceLab mode="rm-bias". Scatter (x = gold, y = RM score), points coloured by candidate type; shaded band = range of lengths seen in training pairs.
MOTION over t: points appear grouped by candidate type; a fitted trend line draws; the off-trend group highlights with --accent at the end.
TRY: find the candidate with the highest RM score and read its gold score.
NUMBERS: rank correlation between RM and gold (in-range vs all); RM and gold score of the top-RM candidate.
*/}

<Step title="Optimising the policy with PPO">

The policy is then trained to produce responses the reward model scores highly, applying Day 11 PPO's clipped objective alongside a separate penalty for moving away from the SFT policy.

WIDGET: PreferenceLab mode="ppo" labels="human" beta=0.1 iters=200 batch=64 seed=6. Top: candidate probability bars for one prompt (SFT reference as ghost bars). Bottom: three curves vs iteration: RM reward (proxy, --viz-forward), gold reward (--text dashed), KL to reference.
MOTION over t: each iteration: 64 sampled responses fly as dots into the batch, get a score, bars and theta tween; curves draw.
TRY: watch gold reward rise along with the proxy.
NUMBERS: proxy, gold and KL at iterations 0 and 200; gold improvement over SFT.

</Step>

<Step title="The KL penalty to the reference model">

<Step title="Reward hacking">

With no penalty, the policy finds the responses that the reward model overrates and puts nearly all its probability on them, so the measured reward climbs while the true quality falls.

{/*
IDEA: Goodhart. The proxy is exploited outside its training distribution.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

Common misunderstandings about RLHF.

{/*
NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
1. "The reward model knows what is good." It predicts labeler choices on the data it saw.
2. "Higher reward-model score is always better." Past some point it means exploitation (Gao et al.).
3. "The KL penalty is just regularisation for stability." It also keeps the policy where the reward model is trustworthy.
4. "RLHF adds knowledge." It reweights behaviours the model can already produce.
5. "Reward scores are absolute." Bradley-Terry scores are defined only up to a constant.
*/}

</Step>
