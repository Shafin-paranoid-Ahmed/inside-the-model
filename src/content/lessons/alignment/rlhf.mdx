People find it easier to rank two answers than to write a perfect one.

<Step title="Comparing two answers">

After supervised fine-tuning ([the previous lesson](/learn/alignment/sft/)) the model answers questions, but "answers" is a low bar. We want responses a person would actually prefer, and writing the ideal response is slow. Judging is quicker: show a labeler two responses to the same prompt and ask which is better. That works even when the labeler could not have written the better one.

A real model writes its response token by token. Here the model picks one of eight candidate responses per prompt, so every number below can be computed exactly. The **gold score** of a response stands for what the person really wanted. Real projects never see it; we can, because we built the toy. The simulated labelers do not see it either. They are noisy, and they like long answers a little too much.

Each square is one pair of responses sampled from the SFT model, blue when the labeler's choice agrees with the gold ranking and orange when it does not. Of 200 pairs, 69% agree with gold. The longer response wins 49% of the time, and in 58 pairs the labeler picked the longer response although it was worse. Click square 3 to see one: for "What is 7 + 5?" the labeler chose the hedged answer, "I think it might be 12, but I'm not completely sure" (11 words, gold 2.17), over "7 + 5 = 12." (5 words, gold 2.85).

<Step title="The Bradley-Terry reward model">

Pairs are only comparisons. To optimise a policy we need a number per response, so we train a **reward model**: a function $r$ that gives every response a score. It is trained so that the response the labeler chose scores higher. The **Bradley-Terry** model says how much higher: the probability that the chosen response $y_w$ beats the rejected one $y_l$ is

$$P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma\bigl(r(y_w) - r(y_l)\bigr),$$

where $\sigma$ squashes any number into a probability. A score difference of 0 is a coin flip, and a difference of 1 is a 73% preference. Only differences matter, so scores are defined up to a constant.

In this toy the reward model is a weighted sum of six features of a response (answers, correct, length, flattery, refuses, hedges), so training means finding six weights. Watch them move from 0. The bars are the learned weights; the black ticks are the gold weights. The weight on correct ends at 2.64 (gold 2.00), which is what we wanted. The weight on length ends at +0.76, where gold says $-0.30$: the labelers rewarded length, so a model that predicts them must too. Its loss falls from 0.693 to 0.487, and on 1000 fresh pairs it picks the labeler's choice 68% of the time. Scoring with the gold weights themselves would match only 65% of the same labels, because gold does not know about the length bias.

<PreferenceLab client:visible mode="reward-model" labels="human" pairs={200} steps={200} seed={4} title="A reward model learns from 200 comparisons" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

The likelihood of the data is the product of $\sigma(r(y_w) - r(y_l))$ over pairs, so training minimises

$$\mathcal{L}(r) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(y_w, y_l)} \log \sigma\bigl(r(y_w) - r(y_l)\bigr).$$

With scores $r(y) = w \cdot \phi(y)$ this is logistic regression on the feature differences $\phi(y_w) - \phi(y_l)$, with no intercept.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
# r: reward model, returns one score per response
r_w, r_l = r(chosen), r(rejected)
loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()
loss.backward()
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="What the reward model learned">

A reward model learns whatever predicted the labels, including the labelers' habits, and it is only tested on the kinds of response it saw. The longest response in the 200 pairs has 28 words. The bank also holds a rambling wrong answer of 48 words: very long, very confident, and wrong. The SFT model almost never writes it, so it never appeared in a pair. The reward model has to guess how it would be judged, and a model with a positive weight on length guesses high.

Each dot is one response to one prompt, plotted by its gold score (across) and the reward model's score (up). The dots appear one response type at a time. Seven of the types lie close to a rising line: their rank correlation with gold is 0.88. Then the rambling wrong answer appears at the top of the plot. Its reward model score is 4.72, the highest of all 80 responses, while its gold score is $-0.44$. Including it, the rank correlation falls to 0.47.

<Step title="Optimising the policy with PPO">

Now the policy is trained to produce responses the reward model scores highly. This is a reinforcement-learning problem and we already have its solution: the policy collects a batch of responses, scores them, works out advantages, and updates with PPO's clipped objective, exactly as in the [PPO lesson](/learn/alignment/ppo/). What changes is the setting. The action is a whole response, and the reward comes from the learned reward model instead of an environment.

One term is new. Left alone, the policy could drift anywhere the reward model says is good. So each response's reward is reduced by how much more likely the policy has made it than the original SFT model did, which is the **KL penalty**. With strength $\beta = 0.1$ the reward of a sampled response is $r_{\text{RM}}(y) - \beta\,(\log\pi(y) - \log\pi_{\text{SFT}}(y))$. The SFT model is the fixed **reference**.

At each update the figure samples 64 responses (the dots; 9 of them for the prompt shown), scores them, and updates. The outlined bars are the SFT model's probabilities for "What is 7 + 5?": terse correct 0.43, clear correct 0.28. After 200 updates the policy writes the clear answer with probability 0.78 and the terse one with 0.19. The proxy reward (the reward model's own score) rises from 3.35 to 4.08, and the gold reward from 2.27 to 2.81. It already reaches 2.63 after 5 updates. The KL to the SFT model ends at 0.802.

<PreferenceLab client:visible mode="ppo" labels="human" beta={0.1} iters={200} batch={64} seed={6} title="PPO against the reward model, penalty 0.1" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

The policy maximises the expected reward minus a penalty for leaving the reference:

$$\max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).$$

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) applies the penalty per token, as a term subtracted from the reward. Here it is applied per response. In place of PPO's learned value network, the advantage subtracts a running mean of the shaped reward for each prompt.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
for update in range(200):
    x, y = sample_responses(policy, prompts, n=64)       # pi_old collects the batch
    logp_old = policy.log_prob(y, x).detach()
    reward = rm(x, y) - beta * (logp_old - ref.log_prob(y, x))
    adv = (reward - baseline[x]).detach()                # fixed for this batch
    for epoch in range(4):
        opt.zero_grad()
        ratio = (policy.log_prob(y, x) - logp_old).exp()
        surr = torch.min(ratio * adv, ratio.clamp(0.8, 1.2) * adv)
        (-surr.mean()).backward(); opt.step()
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Inside one PPO update">

The [PPO lesson](/learn/alignment/ppo/) explained the ratio $\rho = \pi_{\text{new}}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)$ and the clip that removes the reward for pushing it past $1\pm\varepsilon$. Here is that clip applied to responses. Two policies are in play: the old policy that collected this batch, which changes at every update, and the SFT reference of the KL penalty, which never changes. The clip compares with the first, the penalty with the second.

The figure follows the very first batch: 64 responses, 45 with positive advantage. Before the update every ratio is 1. After epoch 1 the ratios span 0.68 to 1.10: five samples are already below the band's lower edge of 0.8. After epoch 4 they span 0.43 to 1.29: 23 of the 64 are outside the band, so the ratio was not held between 0.8 and 1.2. Of those 23, 21 are on the flat side (hollow dots) and add nothing to the gradient. The other 2 are on the side where the objective still pushes. Click a dot to see its own objective curve below; the first one selected is such a sample.

<PreferenceLab client:visible mode="ppo-clip" beta={0.1} iter={0} epochs={4} eps={0.2} seed={6} title="One batch, four epochs, clipped objective" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

$$L = \tfrac{1}{B}\sum_i \min\bigl(\rho_i A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i,\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,A_i\bigr),\qquad \rho_i = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i\mid x_i)}.$$

Which side of the band is flat depends on the sign of $A_i$ (Schulman et al., 2017). Later in training the policy settles and a batch's ratios stay inside the band, so this figure uses the first batch.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The KL penalty to the reference model">

The penalty $\beta$ sets a price on moving. The **KL divergence** measures how far the policy's probabilities have moved from the SFT model's: 0 means identical, larger means further. A large $\beta$ makes every step away from SFT expensive, and a small one makes it cheap. Here the KL is computed exactly, by summing over the eight candidates and averaging over prompts.

Four policies are trained against the same reward model with $\beta$ = 0.5, 0.1, 0.02 and 0. The plot shows gold reward against distance from the SFT model, on a square-root scale so that 0.29 and 13 both fit. The strong penalty stops at KL 0.29 with gold 2.76. $\beta = 0.1$ goes a little further, to KL 0.80 and gold 2.81, the best of the four. The two weakest penalties both travel to KL 13.01, and both end with gold $-0.44$, far below where they started (2.27).

</Step>

<Step title="Reward hacking">

With no penalty, nothing stops the policy from following the reward model wherever it points, including into the region it was never tested on. The rambling wrong answer scores highest, so given enough updates the policy puts nearly all its probability on it. The measured reward keeps climbing while the true quality collapses. This is **reward hacking**, an instance of Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes the target, it stops measuring what you cared about.

Watch the run with $\beta = 0$. Gold reward rises as before and peaks at update 19 at 2.82, while KL is 0.43. By update 20 it is 2.81 and the average probability of the rambling answer is still 0.00. At update 25 it is 0.15 and gold has dropped to 2.31, and by update 30 it is above 0.99 and gold is about $-0.4$. Meanwhile the proxy reward keeps rising, to 4.72. For "What is 7 + 5?" the policy goes from writing "7 + 5 = 12." to the overconfident, wrong paragraph about thinking very carefully. Drag the timeline to update 19 and then to the end and compare the response named under the figure.

<PreferenceLab client:visible mode="ppo" labels="human" beta={0} iters={200} batch={64} seed={6} title="No penalty: the policy finds the rambling answer" />

A related pattern appears in real systems: [Singhal et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716) found that much of the improvement RLHF gives in three settings can be reproduced by rewarding length alone.

</Step>

<Step title="Over-optimisation as a curve">

Plot the same run against how far the policy has moved, and the pattern becomes a curve. [Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760) studied this with a large "gold" reward model standing in for people, and measured how gold reward changes as a policy is optimised against smaller proxy reward models. The shape here is the one the lesson has been building to: gold rises at first and then falls, while the proxy keeps going up.

The horizontal axis is the square root of the KL to the SFT model. Gold peaks at distance 0.65. By the end, at distance 3.61, the proxy is 4.72 and gold is $-0.44$, so the reward model overstates the quality by 5.16. The shaded band is that gap. When it finishes, the end points of the three penalised runs appear: $\beta = 0.5$ ends at distance 0.54 with gold 2.76, $\beta = 0.1$ at 0.90 with gold 2.81, and $\beta = 0.02$ at 3.61 with gold $-0.44$.

<PreferenceLab client:visible mode="overopt" beta={0} iters={400} seed={6} title="Proxy and gold reward against distance from the SFT model" />

In practice you cannot draw this curve, because gold is not available. Teams watch held-out human ratings, response lengths and the KL, and stop or raise $\beta$ before the proxy and the people disagree.

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**"The reward model knows what is good."** It predicts which response a labeler chose, using the examples it saw. Where it saw nothing, such as the 48-word rambling answer, it extrapolates.

**"A higher reward model score is always better."** Up to a point. Past it, a higher score means the policy is exploiting the model: the proxy reached 4.72 while gold fell to $-0.44$.

**"The KL penalty is just there for stability."** It also keeps the policy where the reward model has been tested. The $\beta = 0.1$ run stayed within KL 0.80 and kept its gold gain; the $\beta = 0$ run went to KL 13.

**"RLHF adds new knowledge."** It reweights behaviour the model can already produce. The policy here only chooses among the eight responses it started with.

**"Reward scores are absolute."** Bradley-Terry scores are defined only up to a constant; only differences between responses to the same prompt mean anything.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>1. Two responses have reward model scores 1.2 and 0.2. What probability does Bradley-Terry give that the first is preferred?</summary>

$\sigma(1.2 - 0.2) = \sigma(1) \approx 0.73$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. The reward model never saw a response as long as the rambling one. Why does it still score it highest?</summary>

It learned a positive weight on length because labelers favoured longer answers, and a linear model keeps adding that bonus for lengths it never saw.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What does PPO's clip prevent?</summary>

It removes the objective's reward for pushing a sample's probability ratio further past the band on the favourable side. It does not hold the ratio inside the band, as the ratios of 0.43 and 1.29 in the update figure show.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. With $\beta = 0$, why does the policy end up with almost all its probability on one response?</summary>

Nothing opposes the reward model, and the rambling answer has the highest score for every prompt, so each update raises its probability until it dominates.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. How could you notice reward hacking without access to the gold reward?</summary>

Compare against held-out human ratings, audit response lengths and style, and watch the KL to the reference: a sudden jump with a still-rising proxy is a warning.

</details>

</Step>
