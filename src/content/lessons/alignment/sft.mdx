A pretrained model completes text; SFT teaches it to answer.

<Step title="A base model continues text">

A model trained to predict the next token (the **base model** from the [pretraining lesson](/learn/llms/pretraining/)) learns to continue documents. A question can be followed by an answer, another question, or the next item on a quiz sheet. Continuing the text does not automatically mean following the user's instruction. This figure starts with a model that usually continues the question list.

A real model writes its response token by token. To keep every number in this lesson exactly computable, our toy model instead picks one of eight ready-made candidate responses for each prompt: a list of more questions, a terse correct answer, a clear correct answer, a padded one, a rambling wrong one, a terse wrong one, a refusal, and a hedged correct one. The model's probabilities for the eight come from a handful of adjustable weights, which is all that training will change.

Watch the base model on eight prompts. On every one of them "continues the question list" (outlined) is the most likely response, with probability 0.575 on average. The chance of any correct answer is 0.185 on average, and not one of the eight prompts has an answer on top.

<SFTLab client:visible mode="base" title="What a base model does with a question" />

</Step>

<Step title="Instruction-response datasets">

The fix is to show the model what we want. People write a few dozen to many thousand **demonstrations**: an instruction paired with a good response. **Supervised fine-tuning** (SFT) simply keeps training the model, with the same next-token loss as pretraining, on those pairs.

The content of the set matters as well as its size. The LIMA paper fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on only 1,000 carefully curated prompts and responses and got strong results ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)). A small, carefully chosen set can teach a capable base model how to respond.

Here 24 demonstrations arrive one by one, three for each of the eight training prompts. The tally on the right shows the habits the model will copy: every demonstration answers, none flatters, they average 5.4 words, and 5 of the 24 hedge ("I think it might be 12, but I'm not completely sure."). Look for the orange crosses: 19 of the 24 are correct and 5 are wrong. Demonstration 1 answers "What is 7 + 5?" with 13. People writing demonstrations make mistakes too, and the model will not know which ones.

<SFTLab client:visible mode="data" demos={24} demoAccuracy={0.8} seed={2} title="Twenty-four demonstrations and what is in them" />

</Step>

<Step title="Training on demonstrations">

Training raises the probability of each demonstrated response. The score being minimised is the **loss**: the average of $-\log \pi(y_{\text{demo}} \mid x)$ over the demonstrations, which is small when the model gives the demonstrated response a high probability. It starts at 2.973 for the base model.

Each weight is nudged in the direction that makes the model's habits match the demonstrations' habits. The demonstrations always answer and never flatter, while the base model answers rarely and sometimes flatters, so the weight "Answers" goes up and "Flattery" goes down. Early in this run, Adam moves the weights by similar amounts: by step 10 each has moved about 0.49. Later their paths separate. "Answers" climbs from −2.50 to +0.50 over the 150 steps and "Flattery" sinks to −3.27.

The right-hand bars show the effect. After 150 steps the loss is 1.431. The chance of continuing the question list has fallen from 0.575 to 0.057 on average, and the chance of a correct answer has risen from 0.185 to 0.801. Watch the outlined "Continues list" bar collapse during the first quarter of the run.

<SFTLab client:visible mode="train" demos={24} demoAccuracy={0.8} steps={150} lr={0.05} seed={2} title="The weights move and the answers take over" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For a response $y$ with feature vector $\phi(x,y)$ the model is $\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \operatorname{softmax}_y(\theta \cdot \phi(x,y) + c(x,y))$. The loss over $N$ demonstrations $(x_i, y_i)$ is

$$L(\theta) = -\frac1N \sum_i \log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x_i), \qquad \nabla_\theta L = \frac1N \sum_i \Bigl( \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i)}[\phi(x_i, y)] - \phi(x_i, y_i) \Bigr).$$

The gradient is "what the model currently expects minus what the demonstration shows", so gradient descent moves $\theta$ until the two agree.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
# one SFT step: ordinary cross-entropy on the demonstrated tokens
logits = model(input_ids).logits                  # (batch, seq, vocab)
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, vocab),
                       input_ids[:, 1:].reshape(-1))
loss.backward()
optimizer.step(); optimizer.zero_grad()
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Loss on the response tokens only">

In practice each training example is one text that contains both the prompt and the response, for example "user: what is two plus two assistant: two plus two is four". The model predicts every token from the one before. Should it be graded on all of them? Usually only on the response tokens. The prompt is context we supply at test time, so the model has no need to learn to write prompts. Dropping the prompt tokens from the loss is called **masking**.

To see it, this figure uses a separate tiny language model that predicts each word from the previous word, trained on 16 such transcripts. In the example the every-token run counts 12 tokens and the response-only run counts 6, and across the 16 training examples the averages are 12.6 against 5.9. Grey chips are masked. The bars are each counted token's loss: taller means more surprised.

Then both models train from scratch, and the chart tracks their loss on 4 held-out transcripts, response tokens only. It starts at 3.714 for both. Here masking makes almost no difference: after 100 steps the losses are 2.146 and 2.150, and after 300 they are 2.256 and 2.317. Both curves bottom out near 2.0 around step 25 and then creep up as the model memorises its 16 examples. In this small setting the choice is a wash. Real prompts are often much longer than the answers, which is why the convention exists.

<SFTLab client:visible mode="mask" title="Which tokens count toward the loss" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

PyTorch's cross-entropy skips any target equal to `ignore_index`, which defaults to -100 ([PyTorch docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss.html)). Hugging Face training code uses the same convention: set the label of every prompt position to -100.

```python
labels = input_ids.clone()
labels[:, :prompt_len] = -100          # prompt tokens: no loss
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, vocab),
                       labels[:, 1:].reshape(-1))   # ignore_index=-100
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="It learns a behaviour, not a list of answers">

The weights are shared by every prompt. Pushing "Answers" up so that the model answers "What is half of 30?" also pushes it up for questions no demonstration ever mentioned. SFT changes how the model behaves, not just what it says about the prompts in the set.

Two prompts were kept out of the demonstrations. For "How many sides does a hexagon have?" the chance of a correct answer is 0.224 for the base model and 0.811 after 150 steps of training. For "What is 15 − 8?" it goes from 0.159 to 0.679. Watch the bars: the outlined rows are the base model, and the filled rows grow past them without either prompt being trained on. The prompts start with different response preferences, so the same shared weight changes do not make their final probabilities equal.

<Step title="Imitation caps quality">

SFT rewards matching the demonstrations, including their mistakes. In this model, demonstrations that are right 80% of the time pull the model toward that same rate on the training prompts. There is an exact reason: at an optimum the model's average feature values match the demonstrations' average feature values, and correctness is one of those features.

Three demonstrator groups, 60%, 80% and 95% correct (40 demonstrations each), train three models. The dashed lines are the demonstrators' rates and the curves are the fine-tuned models' chance of a correct answer. They end at 0.602, 0.804 and 0.955, almost exactly on their lines. Along the way the 60% model first shoots up to 0.72, well above its demonstrators, and then falls back to 0.60. The 80% model peaks at 0.83 before settling. Early in training the weight on "Correct" overshoots, but the loss does not reward staying there.

<SFTLab client:visible mode="cap" demos={40} demoAccuracies="0.6,0.8,0.95" steps={300} lr={0.05} seed={2} title="Each model settles on its demonstrators' accuracy" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

At a minimum of $L$ the gradient is zero, so $\frac1N \sum_i \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\phi(x_i, y)] = \frac1N \sum_i \phi(x_i, y_i)$. The "correct" feature is one entry of $\phi$, so the model's expected correctness over the training prompts equals the demonstrations' share of correct answers.

</details>

The exact equality belongs to this model. The broader issue is that copying fluent answers can succeed without improving correctness: [Gudibande et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15717) found much stronger gains in style than in factuality when weaker models imitated ChatGPT. Human preferences provide a different training signal: which response is better ([RLHF](/learn/alignment/rlhf/)).

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think SFT teaches the model new knowledge.** Mostly it teaches a format and a behaviour: answer the question, in this style. The LIMA authors conclude that "almost all knowledge in large language models is learned during pretraining", with only a little instruction data needed to teach good output ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)).

**You might think more demonstrations always help.** Demonstrations are copied faithfully, wrong answers included. A thousand careless examples teach carelessness.

**You might think the loss includes the prompt.** Usually the prompt tokens are masked, as in the figure above.

**You might think SFT uses a different loss from pretraining.** It is the same next-token cross-entropy. Only the data changes: pairs that show the behaviour you want.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>Why can a base model answer "What is 7 + 5?" with another question?</summary>

It learned to continue documents, where another question is one possible continuation. The base model in this figure assigns the highest probability to continuing a list; SFT teaches it to favour an answer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which tokens contribute to the loss in "user: hi assistant: hello"?</summary>

Usually only the response: "hello" and the end-of-text token. "user:", "hi" and "assistant:" are context and are masked.

</details>

<details>
<summary>In this model, demonstrators are right 80% of the time. What correctness do you expect after SFT, and why?</summary>

About 80%. Training makes the model's average behaviour match the demonstrations' average behaviour, mistakes included.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why did held-out prompts improve too?</summary>

The weights are shared across prompts, so moving them toward answering changes behaviour on prompts that were not in the demonstrations.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What kind of signal could push the model beyond its demonstrators?</summary>

A signal that ranks responses rather than copies them: human or AI preferences, or an automatic check of correctness. The next lessons cover both.

</details>

</Step>
