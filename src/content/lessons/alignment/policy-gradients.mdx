What if the agent learns its action probabilities directly?

<Step title="Policy gradients: make good actions more likely">

A policy-gradient method skips Q-values and directly adjusts the policy's parameters so that actions followed by high reward become more probable.

{/*
IDEA: softmax policy pi_theta(a) = softmax(theta); gradient of expected reward = E[r grad log pi(a)]. Exact gradient vs one-sample estimate.
WIDGET: BanditLab mode="softmax-pg" arms=3 lr=0.1 steps=200 seed=5 (bandit with known means so the exact gradient can be drawn).
MOTION over t: each step: one arm is sampled (dot drops on it), its reward appears, an arrow on the logit bars shows r times grad log pi (up for the chosen arm, down for the others); probabilities tween; the exact expected-gradient arrow drawn faintly for comparison.
TRY: notice that a chosen arm with positive reward gets pushed up even when it is not the best arm.
NUMBERS: probability of the best arm at steps 0, 50, 200.
SHOW THE MATH: the log-derivative trick, grad log softmax = onehot(a) - pi. Link /learn/math/chain-rule/.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="REINFORCE and baselines">

Subtracting a baseline from the reward leaves the average gradient unchanged but makes each estimate much less noisy, which is why almost every policy-gradient method uses one.

{/*
IDEA: REINFORCE estimator; baseline b (average reward) subtracted; unbiased because E[grad log pi] = 0; variance drops.
WIDGET: BanditLab mode="baseline" arms=3 samples=400 seed=9 (frozen policy, all rewards positive so the no-baseline estimate is badly noisy).
MOTION over t: 400 single-sample gradient estimates stream into two histograms (no baseline vs baseline) for the logit of arm 1; the exact gradient is a vertical line; running means converge to it in both.
TRY: compare the two histogram widths.
NUMBERS: the two sample variances and their ratio; both sample means vs the exact gradient.
SHOW THE MATH: proof that E[b grad log pi] = 0.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="REINFORCE in the grid">

In a multi-step task REINFORCE weights every action in an episode by the return that followed it, so it needs many episodes, and a baseline helps here too.

{/*
IDEA: episode returns G_t as weights; credit assignment is coarse; learning is slower and noisier than Q-learning on this task.
WIDGET: GridWorldLab mode="reinforce" baseline="both" lr=0.1 episodes=1000 runs=10. Policy arrows drawn with opacity = probability (soft policy sharpening); learning curves with and without a baseline.
MOTION over t: arrows sharpen as episodes pass; the two curves draw; a sample episode replays at each marker.
TRY: watch a cell near the pit whose arrow probabilities wobble before settling.
NUMBERS: mean return at episodes 100 and 1000 for both variants (averaged over 10 runs).
SHOW THE CODE: REINFORCE with a baseline, about 15 lines NumPy.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="One episode supplies a policy gradient">

Each sampled action contributes a change to the policy, weighted by how much better its later return was than the baseline.

{/*
IDEA: combine the bandit's log-policy gradient with the grid's return-to-go; accumulate the episode gradient before updating the policy, holding its parameters fixed during collection.
WIDGET: GridWorldLab mode="reinforce-update" baseline="state-value" episode=10 lr=0.1 seed=13. One trajectory beside a chosen state's logits, action probabilities and signed gradient contributions.
MOTION over t: replay the episode forward, fill returns backward, subtract state baselines, then assemble gradient contributions and apply one policy update; marker labels match these segments.
TRY: select an action whose return is below its baseline and see its probability fall after the update.
NUMBERS: selected state's G_t, baseline, advantage, gradient contribution and chosen-action probability before/after; find a seed with the advertised negative advantage before writing the lesson.
SHOW THE MATH: episode gradient sum_t (G_t - b(s_t)) grad log pi(a_t|s_t). SHOW THE CODE: collect, compute returns, accumulate, update.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Actor-critic">

An actor-critic method combines both families: a critic learns state values and its one-step error tells the actor, a policy, which way to move after every single step.

{/*
IDEA: advantage estimate delta = r + gamma V(s') - V(s); actor update uses delta in place of the full return; lower variance, some bias. Bridge to advantages and PPO in /learn/alignment/ppo/.
WIDGET: GridWorldLab mode="actor-critic" lrActor=0.1 lrCritic=0.2 episodes=500 seed=13. Two layers: critic V as a heatmap, actor policy as arrows; the per-step TD error shown as a signed flash on the cell (--viz-pos/--viz-neg).
MOTION over t: agent moves; each step shows the TD error, the critic cell updates, the actor arrow at that cell tilts; later markers fast-forward episodes; curve vs REINFORCE from the previous step.
TRY: see a positive TD error raise the chosen action's arrow.
NUMBERS: episodes to reach a mean return threshold vs REINFORCE (same threshold, from the sim).
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

Some common misunderstandings about these methods.

{/*
NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
1. "Policy gradients need a differentiable environment." Actually only log pi is differentiated; the reward is just a number.
2. "A baseline biases the gradient." It does not, if it does not depend on the action.
3. "Actor-critic waits until the episode ends." A one-step critic supplies an update after each transition.
4. "A baseline always reduces variance." Its quality matters; probe the chosen baseline's variance rather than promising this for every baseline.
*/}

</Step>
