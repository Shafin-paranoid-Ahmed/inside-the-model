When the answer can be checked by a program, no human labeler is needed.

<Step title="Tasks a program can check">

Some tasks come with an automatic check, an exact final answer for a math problem or unit tests for code, and that check can serve as the reward instead of a learned reward model.

{/*
IDEA: verifier = program; reward 1 if it passes, else 0. No reward model to exploit, but the verifier itself can be imperfect.
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="verify" verifier="strict". Toy task: add two numbers with 1 to 4 digits. A response is generated text: k worked column lines (with carries) then "Answer: N" (src/lib/align/verifier.ts). Shows 6 example responses and the verifier parsing each: extract last line, compare to the true sum; tick or cross.
MOTION over t: each response types in; the verifier's parse highlights the extracted number; tick/cross appears.
TRY: find the response whose working is right but whose last line is not in the required format, and see the verifier fail it.
NUMBERS: none beyond the 6 verdicts.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Many attempts per problem">

The model answers each problem several times; some attempts pass and some fail, and that difference is all the learning signal there is.

{/*
IDEA: sampling G attempts; pass@1 vs pass@k; the policy (tabular per digit count over k worked columns 0..4 and a format choice) starts mostly answering without working.
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="sample" G=8 problems=6 seed=2. Grid: rows = problems, columns = 8 attempts; each cell shows k (number of worked lines) as a small bar and a tick/cross.
MOTION over t: attempts fill in row by row; per-row pass rate counts up; pass@1 and pass@8 readouts update.
TRY: find a 3-digit problem where only the attempts that wrote out their working passed.
NUMBERS: pass@1 and pass@8 over the 6 problems at the start policy.
Say once: the toy assumes each column written out avoids a carry slip; that assumption is the environment, the learning is real.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Group-relative advantages">

GRPO scores each attempt against the other attempts at the same problem, subtracting the group's mean reward and dividing by its spread, so no value network is needed.

{/*
IDEA: A_i = (r_i - mean(r)) / std(r); groups where all pass or all fail give zero advantage and no gradient. Then a clipped policy-gradient update like PPO, optional KL.
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="grpo" G=8 problems=6 seed=2 (same samples as step 2). Advantage per cell as a signed bar (--viz-pos/--viz-neg); rows with uniform outcomes greyed with a "no signal" label; the resulting update arrows on the policy's choice bars.
MOTION over t: rewards turn into advantages row by row (mean line drawn, bars grow); zero rows fade; policy bars nudge.
TRY: count the rows that teach nothing.
NUMBERS: number of zero-signal rows; the largest positive advantage and why (one pass among seven fails).
SHOW THE MATH: GRPO advantage and objective (Shao et al. 2024). SHOW THE CODE: group advantage in NumPy, 4 lines.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Training with the verifier">

Repeating sample, verify and update, the policy learns what earns the reward, and accuracy rises on every problem size.

TRY: compare how fast 1-digit and 4-digit problems improve.

*/}

<Step title="Longer responses emerge">

Nobody asked for longer answers, but because writing the working out makes passing more likely, the policy learns to write more, and more for harder problems.

{/*
IDEA: response length as an emergent result of reward, mirroring the growing chains of thought reported for DeepSeek-R1-Zero. Honest: in this toy the link between working and correctness is built into the task; real models are not given this rule.
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="length" iters=150 seed=5 (same run as step 4). Mean response length (tokens or lines) vs iteration, one line per digit count.
MOTION over t: lines draw; a sample response grows longer at each marker.
TRY: read the final mean length for 1-digit vs 4-digit problems.
NUMBERS: mean length at start and end per digit count.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="When the verifier can be gamed">

A verifier checks only what it was written to check, and if it is too lenient the policy learns to pass the check rather than to solve the problem.

{/*
IDEA: reward hacking against a program. Lenient verifier = "the true sum appears anywhere in the response". The policy discovers the "list several guesses" format.
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="train" verifier="lenient" iters=150 seed=5. Same view as step 4 plus a strict-verifier accuracy line computed on the same samples (true accuracy).
MOTION over t: lenient reward climbs to near 1; strict accuracy flattens or falls; sample responses turn into lists of guesses.
TRY: scrub to the end and read a sample response.
NUMBERS: final lenient reward vs strict accuracy; share of responses in the guess-list format.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Where verifiable rewards stop">

Verifiable rewards only exist where correctness can be checked, so the method is strongest for math and code and gives no signal at all on many tasks people care about.

{/*
IDEA: limited domains. Add prompts with no verifier (for example "write a short poem"): reward is always 0, every group has zero advantage, those prompts never change; also note results on whether RLVR adds new abilities or mostly sharpens ones the base model already samples (Yue et al. 2025, VERIFY).
WIDGET: VerifierRL mode="domains" iters=150. Two panels: verifiable prompts (policy bars change) and unverifiable prompts (bars stay at the start policy); plus pass@1 vs pass@8 at start and end for the arithmetic prompts.
MOTION over t: training runs; left bars move, right bars stay still; pass@k bars update.
TRY: compare pass@8 at the start with pass@1 at the end.
NUMBERS: those two numbers; change on the unverifiable prompts (exactly 0).
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

Common misunderstandings about RL with verifiable rewards.

{/*
NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
1. "A program reward cannot be hacked." It can, if it checks the wrong thing.
2. "Models were taught to think longer." Length rose because it paid off under the reward.
3. "GRPO needs a reward model." It needs only rewards; the group replaces the value network.
4. "RLVR works for everything." Only where answers can be checked.
5. "All attempts teach something." Uniform groups give zero signal.
*/}

</Step>
