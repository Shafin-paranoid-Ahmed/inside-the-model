When the answer can be checked by a program, no human labeler is needed.

<Step title="Tasks a program can check">

In [RLHF](/learn/alignment/rlhf/) the reward came from a model trained on human choices, and the policy learned to exploit its blind spots. Some tasks need no such model. A maths problem has one right answer, and code either passes its unit tests or does not. A short program, a **verifier**, can read the response and say pass or fail, and that verdict is the reward: 1 for a pass, 0 for a fail. Training a model on rewards like this is called **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards** (RLVR); the name comes from the Tülu 3 paper, and DeepSeek-R1-Zero used rule-based accuracy and format rewards instead of a neural reward model.

A real model writes its response token by token. Here a policy chooses one of ten *styles* of response to an addition problem (how many columns of the sum to write out, and whether to give one answer or a list of guesses) and a fixed program writes the text, so every number can be computed exactly.

Watch six responses. The verifier is strict: it reads only the last line, which must be exactly `Answer: N`, and compares $N$ with the true sum. Response 4 has correct working, but its last line holds a list of guesses, so the verifier fails it. Three of the six pass. A verifier checks what it was written to check, which is a strength (it cannot be talked into a pass) and, as step 6 shows, a limit.

<Step title="Many attempts per problem">

The policy answers each problem several times, and some attempts pass while others fail. That difference is the only learning signal there is: no one tells the model which step was wrong, only whether the whole attempt passed.

The figure shows eight attempts at each of six problems. Each cell has a bar for how many lines of working the attempt wrote, and a tick or cross from the strict verifier. Two numbers summarise it. **pass@1** is the share of single attempts that pass: 13 of the 48 shown, 0.271. **pass@8** is the share of problems solved by at least one of eight attempts: 4 of 6, 0.667. Look at $317 + 833$. Seven attempts failed. The one that passed is the one that wrote out all three columns, carries included.

In this toy, every column the policy writes out avoids one carry slip, and an attempt that skips a column adds it without carrying. That rule is the toy's assumption, chosen so that working pays; the learning that follows is real.

<Step title="Group-relative advantages">

How should one attempt be credited? In [PPO](/learn/alignment/ppo/) a learned critic estimated what a state was worth, and the advantage was the outcome minus that estimate. **GRPO** (group relative policy optimization) drops the critic. It compares each attempt with the other attempts at the *same problem*: subtract the group's average reward and divide by the group's spread (standard deviation). An attempt that beat its siblings gets a positive advantage, and one that lost to them a negative one.

At $317 + 833$ one attempt in eight passed. The average reward is $0.125$, and the passing attempt's advantage is $+2.65$, because passing was rare. Each of the seven failures gets $-0.38$. Now count the rows that teach nothing. When every attempt gets the same reward, there is nothing to compare, and every advantage is exactly 0: $3 + 1$ was solved every time, while $7770 + 5739$ and $28 + 68$ failed every time. That is 3 of the 6 problems giving no signal.

The last stretch of the animation shows what the update does on $317 + 833$. The push on a style is the sum of the advantages of the attempts that chose it: $+2.65$ on writing all three columns, and $-1.89$ on writing no working, which five of the eight attempts chose. The update raises the first and lowers the second.

<VerifierRL client:visible mode="grpo" G={8} problems={6} seed={0} title="Each attempt scored against its group" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For a problem $q$, sample a group of $G$ attempts $o_1, \dots, o_G$ with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$. The advantage of attempt $i$ is

$$\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)},$$

and it is 0 for every $i$ when the standard deviation is 0. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) then maximises a PPO-style clipped objective with these advantages and a penalty for drifting from a reference policy:

$$J = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\bigl(\rho_i \hat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_i\bigr)\right] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr), \qquad \rho_i = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(o_i\mid q)}.$$

In a language model the ratio is taken per token. Here each batch is used once, so $\rho_i = 1$ and the clip never acts, and $\beta = 0$. With $G = 8$ and one pass, the mean is $1/8$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{(1/8)(7/8)} = 0.331$, which gives $(1 - 1/8)/0.331 = 2.646$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
r = np.array(rewards, dtype=float)            # one reward per attempt in the group
std = r.std()
adv = (r - r.mean()) / std if std > 0 else np.zeros_like(r)
# every attempt then pushes up (adv > 0) or down (adv < 0) the log-probability of its own response
```

</details>

<Step title="Training with the verifier">

Repeat the cycle: sample attempts, verify them, score each against its group, update the policy. The policy has one table of style probabilities for each digit count, and each iteration uses eight problems with eight attempts each.

Accuracy rises for every problem size. At the start the policy rarely writes working, and strict accuracy is $0.54$, $0.34$, $0.20$ and $0.09$ for 1, 2, 3 and 4 digit problems. Compare the extremes. By iteration 20, 1-digit problems are at $0.91$ while 4-digit problems are still at $0.22$, because a long sum has more places to slip and few attempts pass, so there is little to learn from. By iteration 50 all four sizes are at $0.95$ or more, and by iteration 150 every size is at $1.00$. The bars show why: on 3-digit problems the chance of writing all three columns grows from $0.08$ at the start to $0.98$ at iteration 50. Nobody told the policy to write its working. It found what earns the reward.

<Step title="Longer responses emerge">

No reward asks for longer answers. But in this task, writing out the working makes passing more likely, so the policy that learns to pass also learns to write more, and writes the most where a slip is most likely.

This is the same run as step 4, drawn as the mean response length in words. A 1-digit answer grows from $5.7$ to $10.0$ words (one worked line plus the answer), while a 4-digit answer grows from $8.4$ to $37.2$ words, four worked lines plus the answer. The DeepSeek-R1 paper reports a similar trend in a real model: R1-Zero's average response length on the training set grew steadily during RL, which the authors describe as the model learning to use more thinking time. In the toy the link between working and passing is built into the task; real models are given no such rule and have to find a useful way to think longer.

<Step title="When the verifier can be gamed">

A verifier can only check what it was written to check. Suppose a careless programmer loosens the rule: a response passes if the true sum appears *anywhere* in it. The policy is rewarded for whatever earns a pass, and that now includes writing a list of guesses and hoping the right number is among them.

Here the same training runs against that lenient verifier. The dashed line applies an independent strict check: did the response end with one correct answer? Comparing the two checks reveals the loophole. The solid line is the reward the lenient verifier pays, and it climbs to $1.00$ on every problem size. For 1-digit and 2-digit sums the policy discovers that a list of four guesses almost always covers the slips, so by the end $56\%$ and $71\%$ of its responses are guess lists, and its strict pass rate has fallen from $0.54$ to $0.44$ and from $0.34$ to $0.29$. Longer sums are different: four guesses rarely cover the slips, so working things out still wins, with lists at $2\%$ and $1\%$ and strict pass rates of $0.98$ and $0.99$. Scrub to the end of the 2-digit sample: the answer is a list, such as `68 or 78 or 168 or 178`, which the lenient verifier passes and the strict one fails.

<Step title="Where verifiable rewards stop">

RLVR needs a useful check. A program can check a poem's line count or a plan's budget, but neither check decides its overall quality. In this example, prompts without a checker give every attempt reward 0. Every group then has zero advantage, and, as in step 3, supplies no learning signal.

This run mixes addition problems with two open prompts. The left panel shows the style probabilities on 3-digit sums moving away from their starting outlines. The right panel shows the open prompts, and their bars do not move at all: the policy's change there is exactly $0.000$. These prompts have a separate table of probabilities, so the addition updates cannot change them. A language model shares weights across tasks; whether training carries over to other tasks needs to be measured.

Look also at the bars below. With eight attempts, the starting policy solves $0.65$ of the sums at least once, but a single attempt passes only $0.29$ of the time. After training one attempt passes $1.00$. In this toy every style the trained policy uses was already possible at the start, so training mostly made the right style likely. Yue et al. (2025) tested whether this holds for real language models and found that RL-trained models beat their base models when one attempt is allowed, but that base models reach a higher pass@k when $k$ is large, which suggests that RLVR mostly selects reasoning the base model already has.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think a reward from a program cannot be hacked.** It can, if it checks the wrong thing. The lenient verifier in step 6 paid full reward for guess lists, and strict accuracy fell on short problems while the reward reached 1.00.

**You might think models were taught to think longer.** No reward asked for length. It grew because longer working led to more passes. If working had not helped, the policy would have had no reason to write it.

**You might think GRPO needs a reward model.** It needs only rewards, here a verifier's 0 or 1. The group of attempts takes the place of the learned critic that PPO uses.

**You might think RLVR works for everything.** It works where answers can be checked, which is why it is strongest for maths and code. On the open prompts in step 7 nothing changed.

**You might think every attempt teaches something.** A group where all attempts pass, or all fail, has zero advantage. In step 3, half the problems contributed nothing.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>Four attempts get rewards 1, 0, 0, 0. What are the GRPO advantages?</summary>

The mean is $0.25$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.25 \times 0.75} = 0.433$. The passing attempt gets $(1 - 0.25)/0.433 = +1.73$, and each failure gets $(0 - 0.25)/0.433 = -0.58$. They sum to 0.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do very easy and very hard problems both give little signal?</summary>

If every attempt passes, or every attempt fails, all the rewards in the group are equal, so every advantage is 0. The useful problems are those where the policy sometimes passes and sometimes fails, which is why the 4-digit problems in step 4 improved slowly at first: almost all their attempts failed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Design a stricter check that would have blocked the guess-list hack.</summary>

Require the response to end with exactly one answer on its own line, as the strict verifier does, so that a list of several numbers fails. For code, the analogue is a hidden test set the model never sees, so it cannot write a function that special-cases the visible tests.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why might average response length grow during RLVR?</summary>

If longer reasoning raises the chance of a correct answer, attempts that reason longer are rewarded more often, and the policy shifts toward them. The reward only counts the outcome, so length is a side effect of what works.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Name a task whose overall quality is hard to verify with an exact check.</summary>

Writing a kind reply or judging whether an essay is persuasive. Rules can check individual requirements, but an exact answer key does not capture overall quality. Human judgement or a learned reward model can supply a broader signal, as in the [RLHF](/learn/alignment/rlhf/) lesson.

</details>
