[RLHF](/learn/alignment/rlhf/) needs three pieces: a reward model, a reinforcement-learning loop, and a penalty that keeps the policy near its starting point. Can we skip the first two and train on the human comparisons directly?

<Step title="The policy RLHF is aiming for">

Start with what RLHF is trying to reach. The **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the model we start from (here, the supervised fine-tuned model). RLHF looks for a policy $\pi$ that earns a high reward $r$ from the reward model but pays a **KL penalty**, $\beta$ times how far $\pi$ has drifted from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The number $\beta$ sets the price of drifting: a large $\beta$ keeps the policy at home, a small one lets it chase reward.

This problem has an exact answer. The best policy is the reference with every response reweighted by $e^{r/\beta}$:

$$\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}{Z(x)}.$$

Here $Z(x)$ is just the number that makes the probabilities add up to 1. A real language model writes its response token by token; in this lesson's model it picks one of eight candidate responses, so every probability can be computed exactly. The figure also shows the gold reward, which stands for what the person really wanted. Real projects never see it; we can, so we can check whether a policy is actually getting better.

The bars are $\pi^*$ for the question "What is 7 + 5?", the dashed outlines the reference, and the reward model is the one fitted to the 200 comparisons in the RLHF lesson. Drag the timeline. With a strong penalty ($\beta = 1$) the optimum barely moves; its gold reward is 2.69 and its KL is 0.18. At $\beta = 0.1$, the setting PPO used, the clear correct answer rises from 0.28 to 0.91, the gold reward is 2.81 and the KL is 0.99. The black ticks mark where PPO actually ended; none is more than 0.126 from the bar. Keep dragging: by $\beta = 0.045$ the rambling wrong answer already holds 0.81, and at $\beta = 0.02$ it holds 1.00 and the gold reward is $-0.44$. A weak penalty hands the policy to whatever the reward model over-rates.

<DPOLab client:visible mode="optimal" title="The optimal policy as the penalty weakens" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For one prompt, the objective is $\sum_y \pi(y)\, r(y) - \beta \sum_y \pi(y) \log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$. Write $r(y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + \beta \log Z$ (the formula for $\pi^*$ solved for $r$). Substituting:

$$\sum_y \pi(y)\, r(y) - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \beta \log Z - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*).$$

The first term does not depend on $\pi$. By Gibbs' inequality a KL is never negative and is 0 only when the two distributions are equal, so the objective is largest exactly at $\pi = \pi^*$. $Z(x)$ also depends on where the reward scale starts, which is arbitrary, so only the ratios $\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}$ mean anything.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The DPO loss on one pair">

Solve the same formula for the reward instead: $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. A reward model is trained on pairs with the Bradley-Terry rule: the chance that $y_w$ (chosen) beats $y_l$ (rejected) is $\sigma(r_w - r_l)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Both responses answer the same prompt, so the troublesome $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both rewards and cancels in the difference. What is left needs only probabilities. Replace $\pi^*$ by the policy $\pi$ we are training and we get the **DPO loss**:

$$L = -\log \sigma\bigl(\beta\,[\,(\log\pi(y_w) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)) - (\log\pi(y_l) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l))\,]\bigr).$$

The bracket compares two **log-ratios**: how much more (or less) likely each response has become than under the reference. Call $\beta$ times the difference the **margin**. The loss is small when the margin is large and positive. Its gradient is multiplied by $\sigma(-\text{margin})$, the **gradient weight**: a pair the policy already ranks correctly gets a small push, and a pair it ranks wrongly gets a large one.

Watch one pair, "What is half of 30?", where the labeler chose the terse correct answer over the clear correct one. At the start the policy is the reference, so both log-ratios are 0, the margin is 0, the loss is $\log 2 = 0.693$ and the gradient weight is 0.500. After 40 steps the chosen answer's log-ratio is $+1.01$ and the rejected one's is $-6.60$: the rejected answer's probability has fallen from 0.15 to 0.0002 while the chosen one has risen from 0.26 to 0.70. The margin is 0.762, the loss 0.383 and the gradient weight has shrunk to 0.318. Each step pushes less than the one before, because the pair is being learned.

<DPOLab client:visible mode="dpo-pair" pair={0} beta={0.1} steps={40} title="Forty DPO steps on one pair" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

With $\Delta = \log\pi - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and $m = \beta\,[\Delta_w - \Delta_l]$, the loss is $L = -\log\sigma(m)$ and $\partial L / \partial m = -\sigma(-m)$. So the gradient pushes $\log\pi(y_w)$ up and $\log\pi(y_l)$ down, each with weight $\beta\,\sigma(-m)$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(logp_w, logp_l, ref_logp_w, ref_logp_l, beta=0.1):
    # each argument: summed log-probability of a whole response, one per pair
    margin = beta * ((logp_w - ref_logp_w) - (logp_l - ref_logp_l))
    return -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean()
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Training on the whole preference set">

Now use all 200 comparisons. DPO is a plain supervised loop: compute the loss on the pairs, take a gradient step, repeat. It never generates a response while training and never builds a reward model, so it is cheap: the whole figure is 300 full-batch steps.

The bars show the policy's probabilities for the first question, the dashed outlines the reference and the ticks the optimum $\pi^*$. On the right, the reward-model score and the gold reward use the same axes as the PPO figures, with the DPO loss and the KL to the reference below. The loss falls from 0.693 to 0.487 and the policy ends within 0.005 of $\pi^*$ in every probability: DPO at $\beta = 0.1$ reaches the policy the formula promised. Its gold reward ends at 2.82 and its KL at 0.97, and on 1000 fresh pairs from the same labelers it picks the labelled winner 68.0% of the time.

Notice the detour. Gold starts at 2.27, falls to 1.97 at step 100 (KL 3.55) and settles at 2.81 by step 200. For a while the policy moves toward the long rambling answer, whose reward-model score is inflated, and then it comes back as the other weights catch up. The final policy is good, but the path is not a straight line. The last step of this lesson sets the end state beside PPO's.

<Step title="The implicit reward">

DPO never trained a reward model, yet the policy contains one. The formula from step 2 turns any policy into a reward: $\hat r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$, the **implicit reward**. A response the policy likes more than the reference did gets a positive score. This is what the DPO paper's title means by "your language model is secretly a reward model".

Here we can compare it with the reward model fitted separately to the same pairs. Each dot is one of the 80 candidate answers (10 questions, 8 answers each), with the reward-model score across and the implicit reward up. Scores are only meaningful within a question, so both axes are measured from each question's average. At step 0 every dot sits at zero. As training runs they spread out along the dashed diagonal, and at step 300 the correlation is 0.999. The highlighted dots are the rambling wrong answers, which the reward model scores highest.

The agreement follows from the loss: in this model the DPO margin is the reward model's margin with the weights $\beta(\theta - \theta_{\text{ref}})$ in place of $w$. Both fits learn from the same feature differences in the same pairs. Compare their six weights in the bars. The largest gap is for "refuses", $-3.78$ against $-3.22$. Refusals almost never appear in the pairs, so that weight is poorly constrained and keeps drifting during the 100 extra steps in the DPO run.

<DPOLab client:visible mode="implicit" beta={0.1} title="The implicit reward against the reward model's score" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

In this model $\log\pi(y \mid x) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) = (\theta - \theta_{\text{ref}}) \cdot \phi(x,y) - \log\frac{Z_\theta(x)}{Z_{\text{ref}}(x)}$, so $\hat r(x,y) = \beta(\theta - \theta_{\text{ref}}) \cdot \phi(x,y)$ minus a constant for each prompt. That constant is why we centre each question before comparing.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="DPO against PPO">

If DPO reaches the same optimum, why would anyone use PPO? The two differ in the data they see. DPO is **offline**: it learns only from the comparisons it was handed. PPO is **online**: it samples new responses from the current policy and has them scored as it goes, so it can learn about answers nobody labelled. DPO is much simpler and has no reward model to inspect; PPO needs more machinery but works with any score you can compute.

Here both start from the same SFT policy and the same 200 pairs, at two penalty strengths, and the figure traces them together. Each of the first two panels plots gold reward against how far the policy has moved, $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$. At $\beta = 0.1$ they end in nearly the same place: PPO has gold 2.81 and KL 0.80, DPO gold 2.82 and KL 0.97. At $\beta = 0.02$ both end at gold $-0.44$ and KL 13.0, with all their probability on the rambling answer. Learning offline did not protect DPO: its implicit reward extrapolates length exactly as the reward model's does.

Now look at the last panel, DPO at $\beta = 0.02$. You might expect the chosen responses to become more likely during training. They do not. The average log-probability of the chosen responses starts at $-1.40$ and ends at $-3.86$ for $\beta = 0.1$ and $-31.17$ for $\beta = 0.02$; the rejected ones fall further, to $-15.30$ and $-86.92$. The loss rewards the margin between them. At $\beta = 0.02$, probability moves to the rambling answer, which never appeared in these pairs. Both members of a pair can lose probability while the rejected one loses more.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**"DPO has no reward model."** It has an implicit one, $\beta \log(\pi / \pi_{\text{ref}})$, defined by the policy itself. In the implicit-reward figure it matches the separately trained reward model with correlation 0.999.

**"DPO is just fine-tuning on the chosen answers."** Supervised fine-tuning only raises the chosen response. DPO also pushes the rejected one down, and the weight $\sigma(-\text{margin})$ concentrates the effort on pairs it still gets wrong.

**"DPO always raises the probability of the chosen response."** The loss favours a larger margin over the rejected response; it does not impose an increase in the chosen probability. In the comparison figure the mean log-probability of the chosen responses fell from $-1.40$ to $-3.86$.

**"DPO and PPO give the same model."** They share an optimum in theory. In practice they see different data and take different paths, and at $\beta = 0.1$ the end states differ in KL (0.97 against 0.80).

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>1. Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ disappear from the DPO loss?</summary>

It depends only on the prompt. The chosen and rejected responses answer the same prompt, so $\beta \log Z(x)$ is added to both rewards and cancels in their difference.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. The margin is 0. What are the loss and the gradient weight?</summary>

The loss is $-\log\sigma(0) = \log 2 = 0.693$ and the gradient weight is $\sigma(0) = 0.5$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What happens to $\pi^*$ as $\beta$ goes to 0, and as $\beta$ becomes very large?</summary>

As $\beta \to 0$ the penalty vanishes and $\pi^*$ puts all its probability on the highest-reward response, even if the reward model is wrong about it. For very large $\beta$ it stays at $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Name one thing PPO can do that DPO cannot.</summary>

PPO samples new responses and scores them, so it can learn from any reward you can compute for them, such as a checker for maths answers (see [RLVR](/learn/alignment/rlvr/)). DPO can only learn from the pairs it was given.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. The chosen log-ratio is $+0.5$, the rejected one is $-0.3$, and $\beta = 0.1$. What are the loss and the gradient weight?</summary>

The margin is $0.1 \times (0.5 - (-0.3)) = 0.08$. The loss is $-\log\sigma(0.08) = 0.654$ and the gradient weight is $\sigma(-0.08) = 0.480$.

</details>
