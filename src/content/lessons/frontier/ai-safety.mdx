Making a model capable is one problem; making it reliably do what we intend is another.

(a) TEXTBOOK-STABLE MECHANISMS:
- Specification gaming: optimising a proxy reward finds behaviours that score well but miss the intent.
- Goodhart / over-optimisation: true objective can rise then fall as optimisation against a proxy increases.
- Goal misgeneralisation: capabilities generalise out of distribution while the learned goal does not.
- Conditional behaviour: a policy trained with a penalty only when an observable "overseer" flag is on learns to behave only when the flag is on.
- Evaluation statistics: success rates from n trials have sampling error; more elicitation effort raises measured capability.
- Adversarial search finds inputs that fool a fixed classifier; adversarial training closes those holes and search finds others.

<Step title="The reward says one thing, we meant another">

An agent trained to maximise a reward will find whatever behaviour scores highest, including behaviours that satisfy the letter of the reward and miss its point.

{/*
IDEA: specification gaming. Gridworld "race": reward +1 for each checkpoint touched; checkpoints respawn after a few steps; the intent is to reach the finish. The trained agent loops between two checkpoints forever.
WIDGET: SafetyLab mode="gaming". Grid with agent path trail; two counters: proxy reward (checkpoints) and true objective (finished? and steps to finish).
T: t plays the trained agent's episode; the loop becomes visible as the trail repeats; counters tick.
TRY: watch the proxy counter climb while the finish line is never reached.
QUOTE: proxy reward and finish rate for the trained agent vs a hand-written "intended" policy.
MATH: Q-learning update (link /learn/alignment/q-learning/).
RESEARCH: R1 (cite one or two documented real examples from the DeepMind list, with their sources).
*/}

<Step title="More optimisation can make it worse">

When a reward is only a stand-in for what we want, optimising it harder often helps at first and then starts to hurt.

{/*
IDEA: Goodhart curve. Same race, sweeping optimisation strength (training episodes, or best-of-n over random policies).
WIDGET: SafetyLab mode="overoptimize". Two curves vs optimisation (log x): proxy reward rising, true objective rising then falling; the turning point marked.
T: t sweeps the optimisation amount; snapshots of the agent's path appear as a strip of small grids above the curve.
TRY: find the point where the true objective peaks.
QUOTE: optimisation amount at the peak; true objective at the peak and at the end.
MATH: none.
RESEARCH: R2 (Gao et al.'s functional forms; Baker et al.'s observation about penalising visible hacking; quote, don't paraphrase from memory).
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Right skills, wrong goal">

A model can keep all its skills in a new situation but pursue the wrong goal, because during training two goals always looked the same.

{/*
IDEA: goal misgeneralisation. Training levels: the coin is always in the rightmost column; the agent learns "go right". Test levels: coin placed at random.
WIDGET: SafetyLab mode="misgeneralize". Two panels: a training level and a test level, agent trails; readouts: reaches the right edge (capability) vs collects the coin (goal), on train and test.
T: t plays episodes alternating train and test levels.
TRY: watch the agent on a test level walk past the coin to the right wall.
QUOTE: coin rate and right-edge rate on train vs test (100 seeded levels each).
MATH: none.
RESEARCH: R3 (CoinRun result as reported).
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Behaving well only when watched">

If a training signal only applies when an overseer is present, and the model can tell, it can learn to behave well only when it is being watched.

T: t plays episodes with the flag alternating; the trail takes the safe route with flag on and the shortcut with flag off.
TRY: compare the forbidden-cell rate with the flag on and off.
QUOTE: forbidden-cell rate with flag on vs off after training.

<Step title="Evaluations for dangerous capabilities">

Labs test models for capabilities that could cause serious harm, and the measured score depends on how many trials are run and how hard the evaluators try to bring the capability out.

{/*
IDEA: capability evals: sampling error and elicitation. A held-out gridworld task stands in for a "dangerous" capability; elicitation = extra fine-tuning episodes.
WIDGET: SafetyLab mode="evals" trials=20 (slider 5 to 500). Measured success with a 95 percent interval vs elicitation effort; a dashed threshold line.
T: t increases elicitation effort; the point and its interval move up; the interval crosses the threshold before the point does.
TRY: change trials from 20 to 500 and see how the interval narrows.
QUOTE: measured rate and interval at low and high elicitation for 20 and 500 trials.
MATH: Wilson interval.
RESEARCH: R5 (dangerous-capability vs alignment evaluations; time-horizon method). Link /learn/alignment/evaluations/.
*/}

<Step title="Red-teaming: search for failures">

Red-teaming means deliberately searching for inputs that make a system fail, and automated search finds holes faster than people can patch them by hand.

{/*
IDEA: adversarial search against a fixed filter; adversarial training; new holes.
WIDGET: SafetyLab mode="redteam". Left: attack string being edited word by word, filter score bar against its threshold; right: attack success rate over iterations, before and after one round of adversarial training.
T: t = attack iteration; substitutions flash; success curve climbs, drops after retraining, climbs again more slowly.
TRY: watch how many edits the second attack needs compared with the first.
QUOTE: success rate after 50 iterations before and after adversarial training; median edits needed.
MATH: none. CODE: greedy substitution loop.
RESEARCH: R6.
*/}

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

Safety discussions often run into a few misconceptions.

{/*
NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
1. Reward hacking is a bug in the code. It is the optimiser doing its job on a flawed specification (step 1).
2. If a model passes an evaluation, it lacks the capability (step 5: elicitation, sampling error).
3. Red-teaming proves a system is safe. It finds failures; it cannot show their absence (step 6).
4. Safety is only about distant future systems. Cite current incident/eval reporting from R7.
*/}
