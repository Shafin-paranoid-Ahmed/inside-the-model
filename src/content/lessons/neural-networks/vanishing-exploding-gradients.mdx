<Step title="Thirty layers, one gradient">

At the end of [Backpropagation Through an MLP](/learn/neural-networks/backprop-mlp/) we noticed that every layer between the loss and a weight adds one more factor to that weight's gradient, and that each tanh activation derivative is at most 1 (the weight matrix can still amplify it). With one hidden layer that is harmless. With thirty it can be catastrophic.

The figure below is a real experiment. It builds a 30-layer network with 24 units per layer and random weights, runs an input through it, and then runs backpropagation from the output. Each bar is the size of the gradient $\lVert\partial L/\partial h_\ell\rVert$ at layer $\ell$, on a **log scale** where the baseline is the gradient size at the output. A bar pointing down means the gradient there is smaller than at the output; a bar pointing up, larger.

The activation is sigmoid, the weights are on the standard scale, and watch what the backward wave leaves behind. By the time it reaches the input, the gradient is roughly $10^{-19.5}$ of its size at the output: about twenty orders of magnitude smaller. With ordinary learning rates and float32 weights of order one, gradients this small can produce updates below rounding precision, making early-layer learning ineffective in this example.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="sigmoid" scale={1} title="Sigmoid, 30 layers: the gradient dies on the way down" />

This is the **vanishing gradient problem**. Its mirror image, **exploding gradients**, is the same effect with the factors larger than 1. In this lesson you will see what causes each, and which fixes work. (A note on what this experiment simplifies: no biases, one input, random untrained weights, a width-24 chain. Real networks are messier, but the mechanism is the same.)

</Step>

<Step title="A gradient is a product of Jacobians">

Take the backward step of one layer. Layer $\ell$ computes $\mathbf z_\ell = W_\ell \mathbf h_{\ell-1}$ and $\mathbf h_\ell = \varphi(\mathbf z_\ell)$. By the chain rule, the gradient at the layer's input is the gradient at its output pushed through the layer's Jacobian, transposed:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_{\ell-1}} = W_\ell^{\top}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\varphi'(\mathbf z_\ell)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_\ell}.$$

Every layer does this, so the gradient at layer 0 is a **long product**:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_0} = J_1^{\top} J_2^{\top} \cdots J_L^{\top}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_L}, \qquad J_\ell^{\top} = W_\ell^{\top}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\varphi'(\mathbf z_\ell)\bigr).$$

This is exactly the chain rule you saw with three links, now with thirty and with matrices instead of numbers. Recall what happens to a product of many numbers: if each factor is about $r$, the product is about $r^{30}$. For $r = 0.9$ that is $0.04$; for $r = 0.5$ it is $10^{-9}$; for $r = 1.5$ it is $2 \times 10^{5}$. There is no gentle middle: the size of the result changes exponentially with depth unless the factor sits almost exactly at 1.

The figure below is the stable case, for comparison: ReLU with weights scaled so the average factor is close to 1. The bars stay close to the baseline all the way down. The rest of the lesson is about what moves the factor away from 1, and how to bring it back.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="relu" scale={1.4142} controls={false} title="A stable product: bars stay near the baseline" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

The size of one step depends on the norm of $J_\ell^{\top}$. Since $\lVert J_\ell^{\top} \mathbf g\rVert \le \lVert W_\ell\rVert_2\,\max_i|\varphi'(z_{\ell,i})|\,\lVert\mathbf g\rVert$, if the largest singular value of every $W_\ell$ times the largest possible slope $\gamma = \sup|\varphi'|$ is below 1, then

$$\Bigl\lVert \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_0}\Bigr\rVert \le \bigl(\lVert W\rVert_2\,\gamma\bigr)^{L}\,\Bigl\lVert \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_L}\Bigr\rVert \to 0 \quad\text{exponentially in } L.$$

That is a **sufficient** condition for vanishing (Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013, in the setting of recurrent networks, where the same matrix repeats over time). Conversely, for gradients to explode it is **necessary** that the largest singular value exceed $1/\gamma$. For a random $n \times n$ matrix with entries of variance $s^2/n$, the largest singular value is about $2s$, and for sigmoid $\gamma = 1/4$, so $\lVert W\rVert_2\,\gamma \approx s/2$: the bound predicts vanishing for roughly $s < 2$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Sigmoid: a slope of at most one quarter">

The sigmoid $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ has derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$. That is largest at $z = 0$, where it equals $\tfrac14$, and it falls toward 0 in both directions. So every sigmoid layer multiplies the backward signal by at most 0.25 (times the weight matrix's own effect), and even if all the units sit at the sweet spot, $0.25^{30} \approx 10^{-18}$. If the units are saturated, with $|z|$ large and the slope almost 0, it is worse.

In the figure the readout reports the average $\varphi'$ over units (about $0.23$ here, close to the ceiling of $0.25$) and the **gain** per layer, the geometric mean factor by which one layer shrinks the gradient: about $0.22$.

Larger weights can make up some of the difference. Slide the weight scale $s$ up to 3: the gradient at layer 0 rises from $10^{-19.5}$ to about $10^{-8.8}$, better but still hopeless. Sigmoid pays a $\times 0.25$ price at every layer, and no weight scale with a stable forward pass can buy that back. This is the chief reason sigmoid fell out of favor as a hidden activation in deep networks, and why it survives mostly at the output, where there is only one of it.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="sigmoid" scale={1} title="Try the weight scale slider on sigmoid" />

</Step>

<Step title="Tanh: it depends on the weight scale">

The hyperbolic tangent has slope $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2 z$, which reaches 1 at $z = 0$. So near zero the layer neither shrinks nor grows the signal, and the result depends on how big the weights are. Switch to tanh and slide the scale.

- **$s = 1$ (the Glorot scale):** the gradient at layer 0 is only about $10^{-1.3}$ of that at the output. Almost stable.
- **$s = 0.5$:** small weights shrink everything, and the gradient falls to about $10^{-9.7}$.
- **$s = 3$:** large weights push units into saturation (slope near 0) *and* amplify. Here the amplification wins: about $10^{3.5}$, the gradient **explodes**.

Two competing effects set the factor: the weights push it up in proportion to their scale, and saturation pulls it down. Tanh only behaves well in a narrow band of scales, and a network trained for a while can drift out of it.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="tanh" scale={1} title="Tanh is stable only in a band of weight scales" />

</Step>

<Step title="ReLU: a slope of exactly 0 or 1">

ReLU has derivative 1 for positive inputs and 0 for negative ones, so it never *shrinks* the gradient on units that are active; it just switches some units off. About half of the units are active for a typical random input, so on average a layer keeps half of the gradient's variance, and the per-layer gain works out to about $s/\sqrt 2$.

Check this against the figure. Set ReLU with $s = 1$: the predicted gain is $1/\sqrt2 = 0.71$ and the measured one is $0.67$, which compounds to about $10^{-5.1}$ over 30 layers. At $s = 2$ the prediction is $1.41$ and the measurement is $1.35$: the gradient grows to about $10^{3.9}$. The prediction is exactly $1$ at $s = \sqrt2$, and with that scale the figure shows the gradient at layer 0 only about $10^{-0.7}$ of the output. That is the **He initialization**: choose weights with variance $2/n$ instead of $1/n$ so that the average factor is 1.

The price of ReLU is the dead unit: a unit whose input stays negative has slope 0 forever, and everything upstream of it receives exactly zero gradient (you saw this in the backprop lesson with the second hidden unit). It is a different failure from the smooth shrinkage of sigmoid: gradients don't fade, some paths are cut.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="relu" scale={1} title="ReLU: gain about s divided by the square root of 2" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Why $s/\sqrt2$? Suppose the entries of $W$ are independent with mean 0 and variance $s^2/n$, and $\mathbf g$ is the gradient arriving at a layer of $n$ units. About half of the pre-activations are positive, so $\mathrm{diag}(\varphi')\,\mathbf g$ keeps roughly half of the squared norm of $\mathbf g$: $\lVert D\mathbf g\rVert^2 \approx \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf g\rVert^2$. Then a random matrix with these entries maps a vector $\mathbf v$ to one with $\mathbb E\lVert W^{\top}\mathbf v\rVert^2 = s^2\lVert\mathbf v\rVert^2$. Together,

$$\mathbb E\,\lVert J^{\top}\mathbf g\rVert^2 \approx \frac{s^2}{2}\,\lVert\mathbf g\rVert^2 ,$$

so the per-layer gain in norm is $s/\sqrt 2$, and $s = \sqrt 2$ keeps it at 1. This is the calculation of He, Zhang, Ren and Sun (2015). Glorot and Bengio (2010) did the same with $\varphi'\approx1$ (tanh near 0), which gives gain $s$ and hence $s = 1$, variance $1/n$; their actual rule averages the fan-in and fan-out, $2/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out})$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Exploding gradients, and clipping">

The other side of the same coin. With ReLU at $s = 3$ the per-layer gain is about 2, and over 30 layers the gradient at the input is about $10^{9}$ times the gradient at the output. The first update from such a gradient is a huge step, the weights leap far outside the region where the loss was sensible, and the loss can jump or turn into `NaN`. This is common in recurrent networks, where the same matrix is multiplied at every time step.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="relu" scale={3} title="Exploding: the same product with each factor above one" />

The standard remedy is **gradient clipping** (Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013). If the norm of the whole gradient exceeds a threshold $c$, rescale it to have norm exactly $c$:

$$\mathbf g \leftarrow \mathbf g\cdot\min\Bigl(1,\ \frac{c}{\lVert\mathbf g\rVert}\Bigr).$$

The direction is unchanged, and only the step length is capped. It is one line in PyTorch, `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), c)`. It treats the symptom: it stops a single bad step from wrecking training, but it does nothing for a *vanishing* gradient, because it never makes a small gradient larger.

</Step>

<Step title="Remedy: choose the initial scale on purpose">

Since the factor is set by the weight scale, the first fix is to choose it so that the factor is 1. You have already met the two standard recipes as buttons in the figure.

- **Glorot / Xavier** (2010), weights of variance about $1/n$, i.e. $s = 1$, for tanh-like activations.
- **He** (2015), variance $2/n$, i.e. $s = \sqrt2$, for ReLU.

Try both buttons under the figure on each activation. With ReLU, He is stable (about $10^{-0.7}$) and Glorot shrinks the gradient by a factor of about $10^{5}$ over 30 layers. With tanh in this network both are close to stable ($10^{-1.3}$ for Glorot, $10^{0.1}$ for He), although the theory behind them assumes different things. With sigmoid neither helps. The rule of thumb: match the initialization to the activation. A detailed derivation and the reasons all-zero initialization fails (symmetry) belong to the [weight initialization](/learn/neural-networks/weight-initialization/) lesson.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="tanh" scale={1} title="Use the Glorot and He buttons on each activation" />

Good initialization sets up the first step of training. It does not keep the factor at 1 as the weights change; for that the architecture has to help.

</Step>

<Step title="Remedy: give the gradient a highway">

The strongest fix changes the layer itself. A **residual** (skip) connection makes a layer compute $\mathbf h_\ell = \mathbf h_{\ell-1} + \varphi(W_\ell \mathbf h_{\ell-1})$ instead of $\varphi(W_\ell \mathbf h_{\ell-1})$. Its backward map becomes

$$J_\ell^{\top} = I + W_\ell^{\top}\,\mathrm{diag}(\varphi'),$$

and the identity $I$ provides a direct additive route for the gradient. When the branch derivative is small, this map is close to the identity; it is not a guarantee against cancellation or amplification. Tick the skip-connection box on the sigmoid figure below: the same thirty layers that lost twenty orders of magnitude now end at about $10^{0.1}$, within 30 percent of the baseline. The gradient has a highway straight to the input. (This is the idea in ResNets, He et al., 2016, which made networks of over a hundred layers trainable.)

Skip connections are not a free pass: the identity can make things *grow*. Try ReLU with a skip and $s = \sqrt2$ and see the bars rise. That is why residual networks are combined with normalization layers and a small initial scale on each residual branch.

<GradientDepth client:visible activation="sigmoid" scale={1} skip={true} title="The same sigmoid network with skip connections" />

Other remedies aim to control the factors. **Gated units** such as the LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) route the recurrent state through an additive path, close in spirit to a skip connection. **Normalization** layers (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015; covered in the [normalization lesson](/learn/neural-networks/normalization/)) control activation statistics. They can improve conditioning, but their own Jacobians are part of the backward product: they do not guarantee stable gradients.

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think vanishing gradients mean the weights are too small. Actually** they mean the *per-layer gain* is below 1, which depends on the weights, the activation slope and the depth together. Small weights can vanish, but so can large weights hidden behind saturated sigmoids.

**You might think ReLU solves the problem. Actually** it removes the shrinking slope on active units, but the weight scale still sets the gain (about $s/\sqrt2$), and dead units cut some paths completely.

**You might think exploding gradients are the same as a too-high learning rate. Actually** they are related but distinct: the learning rate scales the step, while exploding gradients mean the gradient itself is enormous. Clipping the gradient fixes the second; lowering the rate only papers over it.

**You might think this only matters for very deep networks. Actually** the effect is exponential in depth, so it is dramatic at 30 layers, but it is also the classic problem of recurrent networks, where "depth" is the number of time steps and the sequence can be hundreds long. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994) showed that it makes long-range dependencies hard to learn.

**You might think it is a bug that can be debugged away. Actually** it is a property of products. The remedies change the factors, by initialization, activation, architecture or normalization; none of them remove the product.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>1. A 20-layer sigmoid network has an average backward gain of 0.25 per layer. How large is the gradient at the first layer compared with the output?</summary>

$0.25^{20} = 2^{-40} \approx 9 \times 10^{-13}$. About twelve orders of magnitude smaller.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. In the figure, ReLU with s = 2 explodes and with s = 1 vanishes. What weight scale keeps the average gain near 1, and why?</summary>

$s = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41$ (He initialization). Each layer's gain is about $s/\sqrt2$, because on average half of the units are active, and the weight matrix contributes a factor $s$ to the norm. Setting the gain to 1 gives $s = \sqrt2$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Gradient clipping rescales any gradient with norm above c down to norm c. Does it help with vanishing gradients?</summary>

No. Clipping only ever makes the gradient smaller (or leaves it alone); it never enlarges a small one. It addresses exploding gradients only.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why do residual connections keep the gradient from vanishing?</summary>

The backward map of a residual layer is $I + W^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\varphi')$. When the second term is small, the map is close to the identity, giving gradients a direct additive route. This helps but is not a guarantee: the branch can cancel the identity or amplify it, and products over many layers can still vanish or explode.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why can a recurrent network suffer from this with only one layer?</summary>

Backpropagation through time multiplies by the same recurrent Jacobian once per time step, so a 200-step sequence gives a product of 200 factors, just like a 200-layer network.

</details>

</Step>
