<Step title="Multiply matching entries, then add">

Take two vectors of the same length. Multiply the first entries together, multiply the second entries together, and add the results. That single number is the **dot product**. For $\mathbf{a} = (2, 3)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (4, -1)$:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 2\cdot 4 + 3\cdot(-1) = 8 - 3 = 5.$$

In general, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n$. The result is one number, not a vector. If vectors are new to you, start with [Vectors](/learn/math/vectors/).

The figure draws two arrows in the plane. On the right, each matching product $a_1 b_1$ and $a_2 b_2$ is a bar (blue when positive, orange when negative), and the third row stacks those bars end to end, so its length is the sum. Watch the timeline: arrow $\mathbf{b}$ turns once around $\mathbf{a}$, the individual bars grow, shrink and change sign, and the stacked bar always ends at the dot product.

<DotProduct client:visible mode="bars" title="Products of matching entries, stacked into the dot product" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([2, 3])
b = np.array([4, -1])
print(np.sum(a * b))   # 5, the same as a @ b and np.dot(a, b)
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The shadow picture">

The formula is easy to compute, but it hides what the number means. Here is the picture. Shine a light straight down onto the line through $\mathbf{a}$, so that $\mathbf{b}$ casts a **shadow** on it. That shadow is the **projection** of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$. The dot product is the length of that shadow multiplied by the length of $\mathbf{a}$, with a sign: positive if the shadow points the way $\mathbf{a}$ does, negative if it points backwards.

Watch the timeline. At the start $\mathbf{b}$ points the same way as $\mathbf{a}$, its shadow is as long as it can be, and the dot product is at its peak of $4.32$. As $\mathbf{b}$ turns, the shadow shrinks. When the arrows are perpendicular the shadow vanishes and the dot product is exactly $0$. Past that, the shadow flips to the back of $\mathbf{a}$ and the dot product goes negative, bottoming out at $-4.32$ when the arrows point in opposite directions. The right panel traces this on a curve, with a dot for the current angle.

Drag the tips of the arrows, or use the angle slider, to place them yourself.

<DotProduct client:visible mode="geometry" title="Turning b around a: the shadow, and the dot product against the angle" />

So a large positive dot product means "pointing the same way", zero means "at right angles", and a large negative one means "pointing against each other".

</Step>

<Step title="The angle formula and cosine similarity">

The picture gives a second formula. If $\theta$ is the angle between the arrows, the shadow of $\mathbf{b}$ has length $|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta$, so

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta.$$

Here $|\mathbf{a}|$ means the length of $\mathbf{a}$. In the figures, $|\mathbf{a}| = 2.4$ and $|\mathbf{b}| = 1.8$, so the peak is $2.4 \times 1.8 = 4.32$, and the curve in the right panel is exactly $4.32\cos\theta$. The two formulas always give the same number: one uses coordinates, the other uses length and angle.

Now divide out the lengths and keep only the angle part:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|}.$$

This is **cosine similarity**. It is $1$ when the vectors point the same way, $0$ when they are perpendicular, and $-1$ when they are opposite, however long they are. Stretching $\mathbf{b}$ to twice its length doubles the dot product but leaves the cosine unchanged. That is why it is a standard way to compare embeddings, the lists of numbers that models use to represent words and images: you care about direction, not size. In the figure below, $\mathbf{b}$ stays at $60°$ from $\mathbf{a}$ while its length runs from $0.6$ up to $3$ and back. The dot product grows and shrinks with it, from $0.72$ to $3.6$, but $\cos\theta$ stays at $0.50$ the whole time.

<DotProduct client:visible mode="stretch" title="Cosine similarity ignores length: only the angle matters" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Rotate the picture so that $\mathbf{a}$ lies along the horizontal axis: $\mathbf{a} = (|\mathbf{a}|, 0)$ and $\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{b}|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. The coordinate formula gives $|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta + 0 = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta$. Rotating both vectors together changes neither the dot product nor the angle, so the identity holds in any orientation. The projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$ is the vector $\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{a}$, and what is left over, $\mathbf{b}$ minus that projection, is perpendicular to $\mathbf{a}$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

a = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
print(cosine_similarity(a, 10 * a))   # 1.0: same direction, any length
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Every neuron computes a dot product">

Here is why this matters. A neuron in a network takes an input vector $\mathbf{x}$, holds a vector of learned **weights** $\mathbf{w}$ and a number called the **bias** $b$, and computes

$$z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b, \qquad \text{output} = \max(0, z).$$

The dot product measures how well the input lines up with the weights. The neuron is a detector for one direction: it responds strongly to inputs that point along $\mathbf{w}$. The bias shifts the threshold, and the $\max(0, z)$ (called **ReLU**) switches the neuron off unless $z$ is positive. Training a network means moving these weight arrows until each neuron detects something useful.

In the figure, the teal arrow is $\mathbf{w} = (1.6, 0.8)$ and the bias is $-0.6$. The shaded half-plane is where $z > 0$; its edge is a line perpendicular to $\mathbf{w}$. As the input $\mathbf{x}$ turns, watch the bars: the two products and the bias add up to $z$. When $\mathbf{x}$ points along $\mathbf{w}$, $z = 2.26$ and the neuron fires. When it points the opposite way, $z = -3.46$, and ReLU clips the output to $0$. Because of the negative bias, the switch-over happens a little before $\mathbf{x}$ becomes perpendicular to $\mathbf{w}$.

<DotProduct client:visible mode="neuron" title="A neuron: dot product with the weights, plus a bias, then ReLU" />

In real models the vectors have hundreds or thousands of entries, not two, and a whole layer of neurons is many dot products at once, which is a matrix multiplication (see [Matrices](/learn/math/matrices/)). The idea is the same as in the figure.

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

w = np.array([1.6, 0.8])
bias = -0.6
x = np.array([1.43, 0.72])
z = w @ x + bias           # 2.26
output = max(0.0, z)
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** the dot product is a vector, like elementwise multiplication. **Actually** it is a single number: you multiply and then add, and the adding collapses everything to one value.
- **You might think** a dot product of $0$ means one of the vectors is zero. **Actually** it usually means the vectors are perpendicular. Both arrows can be long and the dot product still $0$.
- **You might think** a bigger dot product always means more similar. **Actually** it also grows with the lengths of the vectors. To compare direction alone, use cosine similarity.
- **You might think** a neuron "adds up its inputs". **Actually** it adds them up after weighting each one, which is the dot product with the weights, and that is a very different operation.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>Two vectors have dot product $0$. What do you know about the angle between them?</summary>

Either one of them is the zero vector, or the angle is $90°$: they are perpendicular. From $|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta = 0$ we get $\cos\theta = 0$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>You double the length of $\mathbf{b}$. What happens to the dot product and to the cosine similarity?</summary>

The dot product doubles. The cosine similarity does not change, because the lengths cancel in the ratio.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A neuron has $\mathbf{w} = (1, 1)$, bias $-1$, and input $\mathbf{x} = (0.3, 0.4)$. What is its output?</summary>

$z = 0.3 + 0.4 - 1 = -0.3$, which is not positive, so ReLU gives an output of $0$.

</details>
