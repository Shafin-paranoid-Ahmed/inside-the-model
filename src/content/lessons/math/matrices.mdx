<Step title="A machine that turns one vector into another">

A **matrix** is a rectangular grid of numbers. You can read it as a table, but the useful reading is as a machine: feed in a vector, and out comes another vector. The vector $\mathbf{x}$ goes in, $A\mathbf{x}$ comes out.

The easiest place to see this is the flat plane, where a vector is an arrow from the origin and a $2\times 2$ matrix is four numbers. In the figure the faint grid is the plane as you know it. The bright grid is the same plane after the matrix has acted on it. Watch it bend from the untouched starting position to the finished shape. Every point on the plane, and so every arrow, is carried along with the grid.

<MatrixTransform client:visible title="A matrix moves the whole plane at once" preset="shear" />

Two things to notice as it plays. Straight lines stay straight, and evenly spaced lines stay evenly spaced. The origin never moves. A machine with exactly those properties is called a **linear transformation**, and every matrix is one. Almost every layer of a neural network is built from this kind of machine, only with far more than two dimensions.

</Step>

<Step title="The columns tell you everything">

You do not have to track every grid line. It is enough to follow two arrows: $\hat\imath$ ("i-hat"), the arrow one step along the horizontal axis, and $\hat\jmath$ ("j-hat"), one step along the vertical axis. Any vector is just a mix of them: the vector $(3, 2)$ means "3 steps of $\hat\imath$ plus 2 steps of $\hat\jmath$".

Because the transformation keeps grids straight and evenly spaced, wherever $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ land, the rest of the grid follows. So the matrix only needs to record those two landing spots, and it does so as its two **columns**:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat\imath \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat\jmath \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}.$$

In the figure, $\hat\imath$ is the dark arrow and $\hat\jmath$ the teal one. This matrix is a 60° rotation: $\hat\imath$ swings to about $(0.5, 0.87)$ and $\hat\jmath$ to about $(-0.87, 0.5)$, and each grid square is turned along with them. Click another preset below, or type your own numbers into the four boxes, and read the landing spots off the readout under the timeline.

<MatrixTransform client:visible title="Where do the two basis arrows land? Those are the columns" preset="rotate" hideVector />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

The vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$ means $x_1\hat\imath + x_2\hat\jmath$. Applying $A$ and using linearity (the machine keeps sums and scalings intact):

$$A\mathbf{x} = x_1 A\hat\imath + x_2 A\hat\jmath = x_1\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + x_2\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a x_1 + b x_2 \\ c x_1 + d x_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

So $A\mathbf{x}$ is a weighted sum of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ as the weights.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="A few standard moves, and one that loses information">

Certain matrices do things you can name. Try each preset and watch the grid.

- **Scale**, $\begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$: stretch horizontally by 1.6, squash vertically to 0.6.
- **Rotate 60°**: turn the whole plane about the origin. Its columns are $(\cos 60°, \sin 60°) \approx (0.5, 0.87)$ and $(-\sin 60°, \cos 60°) \approx (-0.87, 0.5)$. For a rotation the morph turns the grid by angle, so it does not shrink on the way; every other preset blends its entries directly.
- **Shear**, $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$: slide each row sideways in proportion to its height.
- **Flip**, $\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$: mirror left to right.
- **Collapse**, $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 0.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}$: the odd one out. Here $\hat\jmath$ lands at $(2, 1)$, exactly twice where $\hat\imath$ lands, at $(1, 0.5)$. Both arrows point along one line, so the entire plane is squashed onto that line.

The shaded parallelogram is where the unit square (the square with corners $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$) ends up. Its signed area is the number the readout calls "area ×", the **determinant** $ad - bc$. Scale gives $0.96$, flip gives $-1$ (same area, mirrored), and collapse gives $0$: a flat shape has no area.

That is more than a curiosity. A matrix that collapses the plane throws information away, since many different vectors now land on the same spot, so no other matrix can undo it. Networks meet this idea whenever a layer squeezes many inputs into fewer outputs.

<MatrixTransform client:visible title="Try every preset. Watch the shaded square's area" preset="collapse" hideVector />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ the determinant is $\det A = ad - bc$. It is the signed factor by which areas change: $|\det A|$ is the scaling, and a negative sign means orientation is mirrored. For the collapse preset, $1\cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0.5 = 0$. Rotations always have determinant $1$: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The output is a stack of dot products">

Now the arithmetic. To turn $\mathbf{x}$ into $A\mathbf{x}$, take **each row** of $A$ and dot it with $\mathbf{x}$ (multiply matching entries and add, as in [the dot product](/learn/math/dot-product/)). Row 1 gives the first output entry, row 2 gives the second:

$$A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \text{row 1}\cdot\mathbf{x} \\ \text{row 2}\cdot\mathbf{x} \end{bmatrix}.$$

The figure shows this for the shear applied to $\mathbf{x} = (1.5, 1)$. The two dashed lines drop from the tip of $A\mathbf{x}$ to the axes. The first output entry is the horizontal position of the tip, and it equals row 1 dotted with $\mathbf{x}$: $1\cdot 1.5 + 1\cdot 1 = 2.5$. The second is the height, $0\cdot 1.5 + 1\cdot 1 = 1$. Read the same sums under the timeline, where the numbers count up as the matrix takes effect, and drag the dashed ring to try other vectors.

This is the view that matters for machine learning. A layer with 100 outputs is 100 rows, each a dot product between the input and one row of weights. A matrix multiplication is a whole stack of dot products done at once.

<MatrixTransform client:visible mode="dot" title="Each output entry is one row dotted with x" preset="shear" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 1.0]])
x = np.array([1.5, 1.0])

y = A @ x                                  # array([2.5, 1. ])
same = np.array([A[0] @ x, A[1] @ x])      # one dot product per row
assert np.allclose(y, same)
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Two matrices in a row: composing transformations">

What if you apply one matrix, then another? First $B$ moves the plane, and then $A$ moves the result. The trip through both machines is itself a linear transformation, so a single matrix must describe it. That matrix is the **product** $AB$.

Watch the timeline: the plane first takes on the effect of $B$ (a horizontal stretch), then $A$ (a shear) acts on the stretched grid. At the end a dashed outline appears: the shape you get by applying the single matrix $AB$ directly. It lands exactly on the grid you reached in two moves.

Here $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$, and the readout shows $AB = \begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0.6\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$. Each entry of $AB$ is a row of $A$ dotted with a column of $B$. For example the top-right entry is $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0.6\end{bmatrix} = 0.6$. In words: the first column of $AB$ says where $\hat\imath$ ends up after both moves.

<MatrixTransform client:visible mode="compose" title="B first, then A, is the same as the single matrix AB" preset="shear" firstPreset="scale" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Applying $B$ first and then $A$ to a vector gives $A(B\mathbf{x})$. Write $B\mathbf{x}$ as a combination of the columns of $B$ and push $A$ through it: this equals $(AB)\mathbf{x}$, where column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $B$. Entry by entry, $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}$, the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. Because the transformations compose, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$: the area factors multiply.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0]])    # shear
B = np.array([[1.6, 0.0], [0.0, 0.6]])    # scale
x = np.array([1.5, 1.0])

AB = A @ B                                 # [[1.6, 0.6], [0. , 0.6]]
assert np.allclose(A @ (B @ x), AB @ x)    # one product == two moves
assert not np.allclose(A @ B, B @ A)       # order matters
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Order matters">

Putting on socks and then shoes is not the same as shoes and then socks. Matrices behave the same way: in general $AB \ne BA$.

Use the two buttons under the figure to swap the order: "AB" means $B$ first, then $A$; "BA" means $A$ first, then $B$. In the figure a rotation and a shear act on the plane, and the result of the two orders is visibly different, and the readout gives different products. With the earlier scale and shear, $AB = \begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0.6\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$ but $BA = \begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 1.6\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$. Different matrices, different pictures.

The order is easy to misread because the matrix that acts **first** is written **last**, nearest to the vector, in $AB\mathbf{x}$. Read products right to left. Deep networks are exactly this: a long chain of matrices applied one after another, with the first layer's matrix sitting nearest the input.

<MatrixTransform client:visible mode="compose" title="Swap the order and the result changes" preset="shear" firstPreset="rotate" hideVector />

</Step>

<Step title="Shapes: m×n times n×p">

Real matrices are rarely $2\times 2$. An $m\times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, and it maps vectors with $n$ entries to vectors with $m$ entries. Multiplication needs the rows of the left matrix and the columns of the right one to pair up entry for entry. That works only if the left matrix's number of columns equals the right matrix's number of rows:

$$(m \times \mathbf{n})\,(\mathbf{n} \times p) = (m \times p).$$

The two **inner** numbers must match, and they vanish; the two **outer** numbers survive as the shape of the result. The figure sweeps over the output cells. For each cell it highlights one row of the left matrix and one column of the right, and adds up their $n$ products one at a time; the result cell fills in. Here the shapes are $2\times 3$ and $3\times 4$, so the answer is $2\times 4$: eight cells, each a dot product of length 3.

Now break it. Drag the slider $q$ (the right matrix's row count) away from $n$. The inner sizes turn orange, the sweep stops and the product reads "undefined". Set them equal again and it resumes. In code, this is the shape error you will see most often when building a network.

<MatrixTransform client:visible mode="shapes" title="Inner dimensions must match; the outer ones become the result" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
X = np.ones((2, 3))          # 2 x 3
W = np.ones((3, 4))          # 3 x 4
Y = X @ W                    # 2 x 4: the inner 3s match and vanish
# np.ones((2, 3)) @ np.ones((2, 4))  ->  ValueError: shapes (2,3) and (2,4) not aligned
```

In a network, $X$ is often a batch of inputs, one per row (batch size $\times$ features), and $W$ maps features to a layer's hidden units. One matrix product processes the whole batch at once.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The transpose: flipping rows into columns">

The **transpose** $A^\top$ turns rows into columns: entry $(i, j)$ of $A$ becomes entry $(j, i)$ of $A^\top$. An $m\times n$ matrix becomes $n\times m$. It is the tool for fixing shapes: if two shapes almost fit, a transpose often makes the inner numbers meet.

Try it in the figure below: press **Transpose left**. The left matrix is turned on its side, its shape flips from $2\times 3$ to $3\times 2$, and the two sizes it shows swap. Press it again to go back. You can then use the sliders to make the shapes match again and see the new product.

Two useful facts. First, transposing a product reverses the order: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$. Second, the dot product of the previous lesson is itself a matrix product: for two vectors written as columns, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{b}$, a ($1\times n$) times an ($n\times 1$), giving a $1\times 1$ result: a single number.

<MatrixTransform client:visible mode="shapes" title="Transpose swaps rows and columns, and the shape with them" />

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think a matrix product multiplies matching entries, like combining two grids cell by cell.** Actually that is a different operation (the elementwise product). A matrix product computes a row-times-column dot product for every output cell, which is why the shapes need not be equal, only compatible.

**You might think $AB = BA$.** Actually the order matters. In the composition figure, swap the buttons and both the picture and the numbers change. Only special pairs, such as two rotations about the same point, commute.

**You might think a matrix only rotates and stretches.** Actually it can also shear, mirror and collapse the plane onto a line. Rotations and pure stretches are just the friendliest cases.

**You might think a matrix is just a table of data.** Actually the same grid of numbers can be a table or a transformation, and in machine learning it is usually both a store of learned weights and the machine those weights define.

**You might think a matrix with zero determinant can be undone.** Actually a collapsed plane has lost information: many points landed on one, so no matrix reverses it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>What does $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}$ do to the vector $(1, 1)$?</summary>

Row 1 dotted with $(1, 1)$ is $2\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 1 = 2$. Row 2 gives $0\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 1 = 3$. The result is $(2, 3)$: it stretches horizontally by 2 and vertically by 3.

</details>

<details>
<summary>The columns of a matrix are $(0, 1)$ and $(-1, 0)$. Where does $\hat\imath$ land, and what does the matrix do?</summary>

$\hat\imath$ lands on the first column, $(0, 1)$, and $\hat\jmath$ on the second, $(-1, 0)$. That turns the plane 90° counterclockwise. The determinant is $0\cdot 0 - (-1)\cdot 1 = 1$, as for any rotation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Compute $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$.</summary>

Each entry is a row of the left matrix dotted with a column of the right. Top row: $1\cdot 1.6 + 1\cdot 0 = 1.6$ and $1\cdot 0 + 1\cdot 0.6 = 0.6$. Bottom row: $0\cdot 1.6 + 1 \cdot 0 = 0$ and $0\cdot 0 + 1\cdot 0.6 = 0.6$. The result is $\begin{bmatrix}1.6 & 0.6\\ 0 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can you multiply a $3\times 5$ matrix by a $4\times 2$ matrix?</summary>

No. The inner numbers, 5 and 4, differ, so rows and columns cannot be paired. A $5\times 2$ matrix on the right would work, and give a $3\times 2$ result.

</details>

<details>
<summary>$A$ is $4\times 3$ and $B$ is $3\times 6$. What shape is $AB$, and how many dot products does it take?</summary>

$AB$ is $4\times 6$. It has $4\cdot 6 = 24$ cells, each one a dot product of length 3.

</details>

</Step>
