A plain autoencoder cannot generate, since its latent space has holes.

<Step title="Where do you pick a code?">
To generate new data from an autoencoder you need to know which codes are safe to decode, and a plain autoencoder never tells you.

{/* IDEA: restate the AE failure as the question a VAE answers: choose a known distribution for the codes
    (the prior, N(0, 1)) and train the encoder to fill it.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="ae" mode="sample" showPrior=[true] (the prior curve is drawn on the strip
    over the AE's code histogram; codes are drawn from N(0, 1), not uniform as in the AE lesson).
    OVER t: prior codes drop onto the strip and decode one by one.
    LOOK: how many prior codes fall where the AE has no training codes at all.
    QUOTE: fraction of prior samples on the data for the AE (quality()). */}
</Step>

<Step title="Encoding to a mean and a variance">
A variational autoencoder's encoder does not output one code per input but a small bell curve: a mean and a spread.

{/* IDEA: encoder outputs mu(x) and sigma(x) (as log-variance); the code is a sample from N(mu, sigma^2).
    Nearby codes must therefore decode to nearby, similar points, which smooths the latent space.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="encode" focus=[same point index as the AE focus step].
    OVER t: for the focused point, its bell curve is drawn on the strip; sampled codes stream out of the
    bell and their decoded points scatter around the reconstruction; the other points show their mean
    codes only.
    LOOK: the width of the focused point's bell compared with the gap between the two moons' codes.
    QUOTE: mu and sigma of the focused point; the spread of its decoded samples. */}
</Step>

<Step title="The reparameterization trick">
Sampling is random, and gradients cannot flow through a dice roll, so the VAE moves the randomness out of the way.

{/* IDEA: write z = mu + sigma * eps with eps from N(0, 1). The randomness lives in eps, a fixed input, so
    the gradient of the loss reaches mu and sigma by the ordinary chain rule.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="reparam" focus=[same point].
    OVER t: eps sweeps from -2 to 2 on a small standard-normal strip; a linked arrow scales it by sigma and
    shifts it by mu onto the latent strip; the decoded point moves accordingly. A two-path diagram shows
    the gradient (gradient colour) reaching mu and sigma, not eps.
    LOOK: the readout z = mu + sigma * eps with real numbers as eps moves.
    QUOTE: mu, sigma and z at eps = 1.
    SHOW THE MATH: dz/dmu = 1, dz/dsigma = eps. SHOW THE CODE: z = mu + torch.exp(0.5*logvar) *
    torch.randn_like(mu). */}
</Step>

<Step title="The loss: reconstruction plus KL">
The VAE's loss has two parts that pull in opposite directions: rebuild the input well, and keep every bell curve close to the standard normal.

{/* IDEA: the ELBO = expected reconstruction log-likelihood minus KL(q(z given x) to the prior). The KL term
    pulls means to 0 and spreads to 1, closing gaps; reconstruction pushes codes apart.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="train" beta=[1].
    OVER t: scrubs training snapshots; the code histogram on the strip spreads out to match the prior curve;
    the decoder curve forms; two loss curves (reconstruction, KL) share a step cursor.
    LOOK: the KL curve rising early while reconstruction falls, then both settling.
    QUOTE: final reconstruction term and final KL term (from snapshots).
    SHOW THE MATH: KL for a 1D Gaussian, 0.5 * (mu^2 + sigma^2 - log sigma^2 - 1); the ELBO derivation
    from log p(x) in three lines (Jensen). */}
</Step>

<Step title="Balancing the two terms">
How hard the KL term pulls decides whether the VAE behaves like a plain autoencoder or forgets its input entirely.

{/* IDEA: weight the KL term by beta (Higgins et al. 2017). Small beta: sharp reconstructions, gaps remain.
    Large beta: codes collapse onto the prior and the decoder ignores the input (posterior collapse:
    every point decodes near the same place).
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="beta" beta=[4] with a beta selector 0.1 / 1 / 4.
    OVER t: the decoder curve and code histogram tween between the three trained models (each segment
    snaps its readout to the real model, never a blend).
    LOOK: switch to beta 4 and watch the decoded curve shrink.
    QUOTE: reconstruction error and on-data sample fraction for each beta. Probe: if beta 4 does not
    visibly collapse, pick the beta that does and change the selector. */}
</Step>

<Step title="Sampling new data from the prior">
Once trained, a VAE generates by drawing a code from the standard normal and decoding it.

{/* IDEA: generation = z from N(0, 1), x = decoder(z). Most samples land on the moons. Because the decoder
    is continuous and the code line is one piece, some samples land on a thin bridge between the moons.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="sample" beta=[1].
    OVER t: 300 prior codes drop and decode (same seed as step 1, so the comparison is fair).
    LOOK: the bridge between the moons.
    QUOTE: VAE on-data fraction vs the AE's from step 1; share of samples on each moon. */}
</Step>

<Step title="Walking through the latent space">
Sliding through a VAE's latent space now traces a single smooth path across both moons.

{/* IDEA: the regularised latent space is continuous and has no empty stretches, so interpolation is
    meaningful.
    WIDGET: LatentSpace model="vae" mode="explore" beta=[1].
    OVER t: a marker sweeps z from -2.5 to 2.5; decoded point traces the decoder curve; prior curve and
    code histogram on the strip. Draggable marker.
    LOOK: where on the strip the decoded point crosses between the moons, and how much prior mass is there.
    QUOTE: the z interval that decodes into the gap and its probability under N(0, 1). */}
</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">
A few ideas about VAEs sound right but are not.

{/* NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
    1. The encoder outputs a code. Actually it outputs the parameters of a distribution over codes.
    2. The KL term is just a regulariser added for convenience. Actually it comes out of a lower bound on
       log p(x), the ELBO.
    3. VAE samples are blurry because the network is too small. Actually a large part comes from the
       objective (averaging under the Gaussian decoder and the KL pressure), visible here as the bridge.
    4. The reparameterization trick changes what is sampled. Actually z has exactly the same distribution;
       only the gradient path changes.
    5. Higher beta always gives a better latent space. Actually too high a beta makes the decoder ignore the
       code (posterior collapse). */}
</Step>
