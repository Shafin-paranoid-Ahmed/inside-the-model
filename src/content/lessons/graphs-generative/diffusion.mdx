An image emerges from static, one small denoising step at a time.

<Step title="From static to data">

Every generative model in this day learns the same data: 600 points on two moons. A diffusion model learns it with an odd-sounding recipe. First, destroy the data on purpose by mixing in random noise until nothing is left. Then learn to run that destruction backwards.

The figure plays the whole loop. In the first half, every point is pushed into pure noise. Watch the middle of the timeline: the moons are completely gone, and the picture is a round cloud of random numbers. In the second half, a denoiser walks each point back, and the moons come out again. They were not stored anywhere in the noise. They come from the denoiser, which here is the ideal one, worked out exactly from the shape of the moons. The readout gives the number to compare later: the share of the points that end up on a moon is $100\%$, split $55\%$ on the upper moon and $45\%$ on the lower. At the noisiest moment, $\bar\alpha = 0.00004$: almost none of the original signal is left.

</Step>

<Step title="The forward process: adding noise">

The **forward process** adds noise a little at a time. At each small step every point is shrunk slightly toward the origin and a small amount of fresh Gaussian noise (a bell-shaped random number, drawn independently for each coordinate) is added. Nothing is learned here; it is a fixed recipe.

The figure shows the points while the noise level $\tau$ runs from 0 (clean) to 1 (noise). The plot on the right tracks two shares: how much of each point is still the original data, and how much is noise. The data share falls and the noise share rises. They cross at $\tau = 0.26$, where signal and noise are equal, a signal-to-noise ratio of 1. By $\tau = 0.5$ the data share is only $0.28$ and the noise share is $0.96$, and the moons are already hard to see.

<DiffusionDenoiser client:visible mode="forward" showScore={false} title="The forward process: the moons dissolve into noise" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

One small step turns $\mathbf x_{t-1}$ into $\mathbf x_t$ by shrinking and adding noise with variance $\beta_t$:

$$
q(\mathbf x_t \mid \mathbf x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\left(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf x_{t-1},\ \beta_t \mathbf I\right).
$$

The figure uses the continuous version of Song et al. (2021), with $\beta(\tau) = 0.1 + 19.9\,\tau$, so noise is added slowly at first and quickly at the end.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Jumping to any noise level at once">

You never have to run the noising steps one by one. Because every step shrinks and adds Gaussian noise, and Gaussians add up into Gaussians, the whole chain collapses into one line: a point at any noise level is the clean point scaled down, plus a random noise vector scaled up.

$$
\mathbf x_\tau = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\mathbf x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \mathbf I).
$$

The figure follows one highlighted point. The long blue arrow is the scaled clean point $\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\mathbf x_0$, drawn from the origin. The second arrow starts where the first ends and is the scaled noise $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. The dot where the two meet is the noisy point. As $\tau$ grows the first arrow shrinks and the second grows. At $\tau = 0.5$: $\mathbf x_0 = (0.76, -0.48)$, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = (-1.05, -1.09)$, and $\mathbf x_\tau = 0.28\,\mathbf x_0 + 0.96\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon = (-0.80, -1.18)$.

This is what makes training cheap: to practise at noise level $\tau$, jump straight there. No chain to simulate.

<DiffusionDenoiser client:visible mode="closed" focus={0} title="One point, jumped to any noise level" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

With $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t} \alpha_s$, composing the one-step kernels gives $q(\mathbf x_t \mid \mathbf x_0) = \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf I)$, which is the line above. In the continuous schedule, $\bar\alpha(\tau) = \exp\!\big(-\int_0^\tau \beta\big)$.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="What a denoiser must know">

To undo noise, a model needs one thing at every place and every noise level: which way is the data from here? That direction is called the **score**: the arrow along which the density of the (noised) data rises fastest. Equivalently, the model can guess **the noise that was added**; the guess $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}$ and the score point in opposite directions.

For two moons built from small Gaussian blobs, the score has a closed form, so the figure shows the ideal answer. Look at the white-ringed probe at $(1.5, 0)$, to the right of the upper moon. At the start of the timeline (high noise) its score is $(-1.50, 0.00)$, which is just $-\mathbf x$: pure noise carries no memory of the moons, so the arrow points at the centre. Scrub to about $\tau = 0.1$ and the same spot reads $(1.26, -1.20)$, pointing down and to the right, at the nearby curve of the lower moon, with predicted noise $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon} = (-0.41, 0.39)$. Hover the plot to move the probe.

<DiffusionDenoiser client:visible mode="field" title="Which way is the data, at each noise level?" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

By Tweedie's formula, the best guess of the noise is the score scaled by the noise level, $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x_\tau) = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\;\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_\tau(\mathbf x)$. This is the same idea as the arrows of the denoising autoencoder in [the autoencoders lesson](/learn/graphs-generative/autoencoders/): a model that cleans up noisy inputs has implicitly learned the direction toward the data.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Training a network to predict the noise">

A real diffusion model does not know the moons in closed form. It is a neural network trained on one simple task: given a noisy point and its noise level, guess the noise that was added. Each training step picks a data point, a noise level and a noise vector, builds the noisy point with the jump from the previous step, and nudges the network to predict the noise vector. It is plain regression, with no opponent and no sampling inside the loss.

The network here is a small [MLP](/learn/neural-networks/mlp/) (two hidden layers of 64 units) trained on the 600 points for 1,800 updates, in your browser when the page loaded. Scrub through the saved snapshots. Thin grey arrows are the exact denoising directions at $\tau = 0.3$; pink arrows are what the network predicts. Untrained, they are unrelated, an average angle of $89^\circ$ apart. After training the angle is $5^\circ$. The loss falls from $0.62$ to $0.43$, and does not go to zero: the best any denoiser can do is $0.39$, because noise can never be recovered exactly from a noisy point. Switch the arrows to $\tau = 0.1$, close to clean data, and the network is still $31^\circ$ off on average. Fine detail near the moons is the hard part.

<DiffusionDenoiser client:visible mode="train" denoiser="learned" title="The network's arrows against the exact ones, as it trains" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

$$
L = \mathbb E_{\tau,\ \mathbf x_0,\ \boldsymbol\varepsilon}\ \big\|\boldsymbol\varepsilon - \hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}_\theta(\mathbf x_\tau,\ \tau)\big\|^2, \qquad \mathbf x_\tau = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\mathbf x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_\tau}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon .
$$

</details>

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
def train_step(model, x0, opt):
    tau = torch.rand(x0.shape[0], 1)                     # a random noise level per example
    abar = torch.exp(-(0.1 * tau + 0.5 * 19.9 * tau**2)) # the schedule
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                           # the noise to be predicted
    xt = abar.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - abar).sqrt() * eps      # jump straight to level tau
    loss = ((model(xt, tau) - eps) ** 2).mean()          # guess the noise
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Reverse sampling: denoising step by step">

To generate, start from pure noise and repeatedly move a little along the predicted denoising direction, lowering the noise level step by step until clean points remain. There are two ways to do this. The **deterministic** sampler (the probability-flow ODE, close to DDIM) follows the arrows exactly, so the same noise always gives the same result. The **stochastic** sampler (an SDE, close to DDPM) adds a little fresh noise at every step, so paths wobble.

The figure starts 600 fresh noise points and walks them with the trained network, with short trails. Colour shows which moon a point ends on. After the walk, $67\%$ of the points are on the moons with the deterministic sampler ($60\%$ on the upper moon, $40\%$ on the lower) and $64\%$ with the stochastic one. The ideal denoiser from step 1 put essentially every point on a moon; a network trained for under four seconds on 600 points is visibly worse. The deterministic paths are smooth curves; switch to stochastic and they jitter, but the result is no better. These numbers are the baseline for the flow-matching lesson.

<Step title="From noise to an image and back">

Nothing in this recipe cares that the data is two-dimensional. An image is a point with many coordinates, one per pixel, and noising and denoising act on all pixels at once. The figure uses tiny 8 by 8 images of the digits 0 to 9, so the ideal denoiser can again be written down exactly. In real models the data is millions of images and the denoiser is a learned network, which can produce images it was never shown; this ideal one can only mix the ten digits it knows.

Three panels show the noisy image, the denoiser's best guess of the clean image, and the noise in the image. The bars underneath show how much the guess leans on each digit. In the first half a 3 is buried in noise: the guess keeps recognising the 3 until $\tau = 0.41$, after which the bars flatten out. In the second half, fresh static is denoised. The guess is blurry at first, an average of all digits, then at $\tau = 0.56$ it settles on a single digit, here a 7, while the image on the left still looks like static. The 7 is a new sample, not the 3 that was erased.

<Step title="Guidance: steering what gets generated">

Text-to-image models take a condition, such as a caption, and **guidance** controls how strongly the samples follow it. Here the condition is a label: "upper moon" or "lower moon". Classifier-free guidance runs the denoiser twice, once with the label and once without, and exaggerates the difference. With strength $w$:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}_w = (1+w)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x \mid c) - w\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x).
$$

At $w = 0$ this is plain conditional sampling. The figure starts from the same noise every time. At $w = 0$, all samples land on the upper moon, spread along it by $7.3$ positions (out of 26). At $w = 7$ the spread shrinks to $2.9$ positions, the samples bunch up near the end of the moon farthest from the other one, and $3\%$ now miss the moon altogether. Stronger guidance buys closeness to the label at the price of variety, and eventually of quality.

</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

**You might think the model removes the noise from an image in one shot.** Actually it takes many small steps. A one-shot guess is a blurry average over everything the image could be, like the early guess panel in the digit figure.

**You might think the forward process is learned.** Actually it is a fixed recipe. Only the denoiser is trained.

**You might think the network predicts the clean image.** In this lesson (and in DDPM) it predicts the noise. Predicting the clean image or the score is an equivalent choice, since each can be computed from the others.

**You might think generation reverses the exact noise that was added.** Actually the starting noise is fresh each time. The model makes new samples; the digit figure ends on a 7 after erasing a 3.

**You might think more guidance is always better.** Actually high guidance narrows the samples and can push them off the data, as in the last figure.

</Step>

<Step title="Check yourself">

<details>
<summary>1. Why does the one-line jump make training efficient?</summary>
You can build a noisy example at any noise level directly from a clean one, without simulating the chain of small steps. Every training step can use a different random level.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. At $\tau$ near 1, where do the denoising arrows point, and why?</summary>
Toward the centre: the noisy point carries almost no information about which moon it came from, so the best guess is the average of the data, near the origin.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What is the difference between the ODE and SDE samplers in what you saw?</summary>
The ODE follows the arrows exactly: smooth paths, same noise gives the same sample. The SDE adds fresh noise each step: jittery paths. Both ended with about two thirds of the points on the moons.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Write the guided prediction for $w = 0$ and $w = 1$.</summary>
$w = 0$: $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x \mid c)$, plain conditional sampling. $w = 1$: $2\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x \mid c) - \hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}(\mathbf x)$.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why can a perfect denoiser on a small training set only reproduce training examples?</summary>
With only ten images to be explained, the best guess of the clean image is always a mixture of those ten, so generation can only end on one of them. Real models generalise because they are imperfect, smooth estimators trained on huge datasets.
</details>

</Step>
