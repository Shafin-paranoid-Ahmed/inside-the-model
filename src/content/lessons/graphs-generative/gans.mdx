<Step title="A forger and a detective">
A GAN trains two networks against each other: one makes fake points, the other tries to tell them from real ones.

    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="duel" run="stable".
    OVER t: scrubs the whole training run; generated points move from a blob at the centre onto the moons
    while the background map shifts; a step counter.
    LOOK: the background map near the end: almost uniform, because the detective can no longer tell.
    QUOTE: final on-data fraction and moon shares (quality()), and D's average output on real and on fake
    points at the end. */}
</Step>

<Step title="The generator: noise in, data out">
The generator is an ordinary network that turns a random input into a point, and training bends that mapping until its outputs look like data.

{/* IDEA: G maps noise z (2 numbers from a standard normal) to x; the whole distribution of outputs is what
    is learned. It never sees real data directly; it only learns through D.
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="generator" (trained G; a small noise panel beside the data plane).
    OVER t: a grid of noise points and its grid lines in the noise panel flow through G to their outputs,
    so the reader sees the square grid folded onto the moons; loop.
    LOOK: which part of the noise square lands between the moons (stretched grid cells).
    QUOTE: the fraction of the noise grid that maps onto the data. */}
</Step>

<Step title="The discriminator: a learned real-or-fake score">
The discriminator is a classifier that outputs the probability that a point is real.

{/* IDEA: D is logistic regression with a hidden layer (link /learn/foundations/logistic-regression/);
    trained with cross-entropy on real (label 1) vs generated (label 0).
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="discriminator" (G frozen at its random start, only D trains for a few hundred
    steps).
    OVER t: the background map sharpens from flat 0.5 into a "real" region around the moons and a "fake"
    region around the generator's blob; accuracy readout.
    LOOK: the boundary where D(x) = 0.5.
    QUOTE: D's accuracy after its training segment. */}
</Step>

<Step title="The minimax objective">
Both networks share one score: the discriminator tries to push it up and the generator tries to push it down.

{/* IDEA: min over G, max over D of E[log D(x)] + E[log(1 - D(G(z)))]; at the ideal D the game measures the
    Jensen-Shannon divergence; equilibrium D = 0.5 everywhere. In practice G maximises log D(G(z))
    (non-saturating loss) because the original gives vanishing gradients early.
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="objective" run="stable".
    OVER t: scrubs training; loss curves for D and G with a reference line at log 4 (the value at D = 0.5),
    with the plane above sharing the step cursor.
    LOOK: the two curves do not go down together the way a normal loss does.
    QUOTE: D and G loss at the start and end, and log 4 = 1.386.
    SHOW THE MATH: the minimax value, the optimal D for fixed G, the non-saturating swap.
    SHOW THE CODE: a 12-line PyTorch training step (D step, then G step). */}
</Step>

<Step title="How the detective steers the forger">
The generator learns from the discriminator's gradient: each fake point is nudged in the direction that makes it look more real.

{/* IDEA: the gradient of log D(x) at each generated point is the direction G is pushed; backprop carries it
    into G's weights.
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="gradients" (one mid-training snapshot, chosen in the probe).
    OVER t: arrows on generated points (gradient colour) grow in; then the points move one exaggerated
    update along them; loop.
    LOOK: arrows near the empty region between the moons point toward the moons.
    QUOTE: the snapshot's step number and the mean arrow length before vs after the update. */}
</Step>

<Step title="Training dynamics and instability">
Because each network chases a moving target, GAN training can circle or oscillate instead of settling.

{/* IDEA: no single loss goes down; the game can rotate around the equilibrium. Larger learning rates
    make it worse.
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="duel" run="unstable" with a learning-rate selector [stable rate, 4x] (exact
    values from the probe) and a distance-to-data curve over training.
    OVER t: scrubs the unstable run; the generated cloud swings between regions.
    LOOK: the distance curve going up and down instead of settling.
    QUOTE: the distance-to-data curve's min and max in the second half of training for both rates.
    Probe first; if no oscillation appears, say what the sim shows instead. */}
</Step>

<Step title="Mode collapse">
A generator can fool the discriminator by producing only one kind of output, and then it stops covering the data.

{/* IDEA: mode collapse: G puts all its mass on one moon (or part of one); D eventually notices, G jumps,
    and the cycle repeats.
    WIDGET: GANDuel mode="collapse" run="collapse" with per-moon share bars.
    OVER t: scrubs the collapsing run; the generated cloud sits on one moon; share bars.
    LOOK: the moon share bars.
    QUOTE: per-moon share at the end of the collapsing run vs the stable run.
    RISK: two moons may not collapse reliably at this size. If no honest configuration is found, ask the
    coordinator before switching to a ring-of-Gaussians dataset (see plan, open questions). */}
</Step>

<Step title="Common misconceptions">
A few ideas about GANs sound right but are not.

{/* NO WIDGET. Misconceptions:
    1. A falling generator loss means better samples. Actually the losses of a two-player game do not
       track sample quality.
    2. The generator copies training examples. Actually it never sees them, only D's gradient.
    3. The discriminator should be as strong as possible. Actually a perfect D gives the original G loss
       almost no gradient (why the non-saturating loss is used).
    4. Mode collapse means the generator outputs one single point. Actually it usually covers a subset
       of the modes.
    5. GANs are obsolete. Actually adversarial losses still appear inside other systems (for example
       GAN-style losses in image autoencoders); VERIFY a concrete cited example before writing it. */}
</Step>
