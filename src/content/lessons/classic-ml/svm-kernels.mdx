<Step title="Many lines fit. Which one is safest?">

Twenty points, two classes, and a gap between them. Any number of straight lines put class 1 on one side and class 0 on the other, so a plain classifier such as [logistic regression](/learn/foundations/logistic-regression/) has no reason to prefer one over another. A **support vector machine** (SVM) does have a preference: pick the line with the widest safety margin, the one that stays as far as possible from the nearest points on both sides.

Watch the first three seconds. Nine separating lines appear one after another, and the readout gives each line's **margin**, the width of the empty street you can fit around it before touching a point. They run from 0.06, where the line almost grazes the data, to 0.57. Then the narrowest line starts to move: training pushes it away from its nearest points and the street widens to 0.58, the widest possible. The plot on the right tracks the width over time.

<SVMMargin client:visible title="Nine lines that separate, and the one that leaves the most room" />

</Step>

<Step title="The margin is set by a few points">

The solid line is the decision boundary $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$. The dashed lines are $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = \pm 1$. Fixing the "1" is only a choice of scale, and it makes the street between them exactly $2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ wide. So **maximizing the margin means minimizing $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$**, subject to every point staying outside the street.

At the end, three points sit exactly on the dashed lines. They are the **support vectors**, ringed in the figure. Every other point is farther out, and the solution would not change if you moved it a little, or deleted it. Press the button under the figure: it retrains on only those three points, starting again from the narrow line, and arrives at the same margin of 0.58. This is why the model is called a support vector machine: the boundary is held up by a handful of points, not by the whole dataset.

<SVMMargin client:visible title="Delete every point except the support vectors: nothing changes" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

The hard-margin problem is
$$\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1\ \text{ for all } i,$$
with labels $y_i = \pm 1$. A point at distance $d$ from the line has $y(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b) = d\,\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, so the closest points are at distance $1/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ on each side. The Lagrange dual is
$$\max_{\alpha\ge 0}\ \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\,y_i y_j\,(\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j), \qquad \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0,$$
and $\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$. Only support vectors have $\alpha_i > 0$. Note that the data enter the dual only through dot products $\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j$. That fact is the key to the kernel trick below.

In the figure the bias is folded in as a constant feature of size 3, which penalizes it very slightly; the widths quoted here come from that formulation. The solution is found with dual coordinate descent, and the animated path is subgradient descent on the objective with the Polyak step size, blended onto that solution for the last 30% of the path.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="Soft margin: allow a few mistakes, and pay for them">

Real classes overlap, and then no street is empty. The **soft margin** SVM lets points fall inside the street, or even on the wrong side, and charges each one a **hinge loss**:

$$\ell(m) = \max(0,\ 1 - m), \qquad m = y\,(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b).$$

A point with margin $m \ge 1$ is comfortably outside and costs nothing. At $m = 0$, on the boundary, it costs 1, and the cost grows linearly as it goes deeper into the wrong side. The full objective is $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \ell(m_i)$. The knob **C** says how much a violation hurts relative to a wide street.

Here 28 points overlap. At the start $\mathbf{w} = 0$, so every point has margin 0 and loss 1. In the right panel each point is a dot on the hinge curve at its own margin; watch them slide right, toward the flat part, as training runs. Then drag C. At $C = 0.1$ violations are cheap: the street is 1.83 wide and 24 points are inside it or past it. At $C = 1$ the street is 0.83 wide with 10 such points. At $C = 100$ it shrinks to 0.42 with 4, and the line bends to fit the stragglers as tightly as it can.

<SVMMargin client:visible mode="soft" title="The C slider: wide street with violations, or narrow street without" />

<details>
<summary>Show the code</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def svm_subgradient(X, y, C=1.0, lr=0.01, steps=2000):
    w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        m = y * (X @ w + b)
        viol = m < 1                          # points inside the margin
        gw = w - C * (y[viol, None] * X[viol]).sum(0)
        gb = -C * y[viol].sum()
        w -= lr * gw / len(X)
        b -= lr * gb / len(X)
    return w, b
```

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="When no line works, lift the data">

A line cannot separate this one-dimensional dataset: class 1 sits in the middle of the number line and class 0 on both sides. The best single cut gets 71% of the 28 points right, and no cut does better.

The fix is to give each point a second coordinate, $\varphi(x) = (x,\ x^2)$. Watch the timeline: the middle points barely move, because their $x^2$ is small, and the outer points rise onto the parabola. Now a single flat line separates the classes, and the SVM picks the widest one, with margin 0.82 and three support vectors. Where that flat line crosses the parabola, at $x = -0.80$ and $x = 0.66$, the dashed guides carry it back down: one straight cut in the lifted plane is two cuts on the original line, with class 1 between them.

The same idea works in two dimensions: a ring of one class around a blob of the other cannot be cut by a line, but adding $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ as a third coordinate lifts the ring above the blob, and a flat plane separates them. A linear boundary in the lifted space is a curved boundary back home.

<SVMMargin client:visible mode="lift" title="Lift (x) to (x, x squared): one flat cut becomes two cuts" />

</Step>

<Step title="The kernel trick: the dot product without the lift">

Lifting has a cost. With many features and a high-degree lift, the list of lifted coordinates explodes, and we would have to compute every one. But the dual only needs dot products between lifted points, $\varphi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\varphi(\mathbf{z})$. A **kernel** is a function $k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})$ that returns that number directly, without building $\varphi$.

The example is the degree-2 polynomial kernel $k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = (\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2$ in two dimensions, whose lift is $\varphi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2)$. The figure fixes $\mathbf{x} = (0.8, 0.45)$, so $\varphi(\mathbf{x}) = (0.64, 0.51, 0.2025)$, and lets $\mathbf{z}$ (length 1) circle around it. The bars show the three lifted features of each vector and their products. Their sum, $\varphi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\varphi(\mathbf{z})$, matches $(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2$ to four decimals at every angle: 0.8425 when $\mathbf{z}$ is aligned with $\mathbf{x}$, 0.4213 at 45 degrees, exactly 0 when they are orthogonal. Drag on the plane to place $\mathbf{z}$ yourself.

<SVMMargin client:visible mode="identity" title="Two routes to the same number: k(x, z) and the dot product of the lifts" />

<details>
<summary>Show the math</summary>

Expand the square:
$$(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z})^2 = (x_1z_1 + x_2z_2)^2 = x_1^2 z_1^2 + 2\,x_1x_2\,z_1z_2 + x_2^2 z_2^2.$$
Group the three terms as products of matching coordinates and you get $\varphi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\varphi(\mathbf{z})$ with $\varphi$ as above, where the $\sqrt2$ factors multiply to the 2. In $d$ dimensions the degree-2 lift has about $d^2/2$ coordinates, while the kernel costs one dot product of length $d$. Training and prediction only ever use $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i\,k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x})$, so the lift is never built.

</details>

</Step>

<Step title="The RBF kernel: smooth or wiggly, set by gamma">

The most used kernel is the **radial basis function** (RBF) kernel,

$$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = \exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rVert^2\big).$$

It equals 1 when the points coincide and falls toward 0 with distance. Its lift $\varphi$ has infinitely many coordinates, so it could never be computed, yet $k$ costs one subtraction and one exponential. The decision function is a weighted sum of bumps centred on the support vectors: $f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\,k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}) + b$.

**Gamma** sets how far each bump reaches. The right panel shows the bump against distance; its dashed mark is the half-reach $\sqrt{\ln 2/\gamma}$, where the kernel drops to 0.5. The data are 42 points on two rings, class 1 inside and class 0 outside, with two deliberately mislabelled points. At $\gamma = 0.2$ the bumps are so broad that the regions are nearly flat and only 60% of the points are right. At $\gamma = 1$ there are 14 support vectors and the boundary is a smooth closed loop that ignores the two bad labels, for 95%. By $\gamma = 10$ the boundary has grown pockets around the two mislabelled points: 100% training accuracy, 31 support vectors. The model has started to memorize noise. Try the XOR data too.

<Step title="Common misconceptions">

- **You might think** the SVM uses all the training points to place its boundary. **Actually** only the support vectors matter; delete the rest and the line stays put.
- **You might think** a kernel computes a new, better set of features. **Actually** it computes a dot product in a feature space that is never built. Nothing is lifted in memory.
- **You might think** a larger C is better because it fits more points. **Actually** C trades street width against violations. Too large and the boundary chases noise, too small and it ignores real structure.
- **You might think** the RBF kernel always finds the real boundary if gamma is large enough. **Actually** a large gamma memorizes the training set, including its mislabelled points, and generalizes poorly. In real use gamma and C are chosen by validation.

</Step>

<details>
<summary>The margin of a hard-margin SVM is 0.58. What is $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$?</summary>

The margin is $2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, so $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 2/0.58 \approx 3.4$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A point has margin $m = -0.5$. What is its hinge loss?</summary>

$\max(0, 1 - (-0.5)) = 1.5$.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Would you increase or decrease C for noisy, heavily overlapping data?</summary>

Decrease it. A small C tolerates violations and keeps a wide street, which is less sensitive to a few noisy points.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does the kernel trick work for SVMs but not for any model?</summary>

The SVM dual and its predictions use the data only through dot products $\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j$, so replacing them with $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$ is all that is needed. A model that needs the lifted coordinates themselves cannot use it.

</details>

</Step>
